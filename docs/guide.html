Design guide · v1.7b.10

Why the engine works as it does.

The design choices behind the two projects, the reader questions, approval, evidence and delivery. Also available as Markdown.

Summary

This guide was written with Claude Opus 5.5.

Research Project Starter 1.7b.10 is two sets of project instructions for a hosted assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Mistral Vibe. The generation project agrees a research question with you and issues a Research Boundaries (RB) file. The execution project takes that file and researches it, and delivers a report to read, its Markdown source and the unchanged RB.

This guide explains the design choices behind that workflow: why the work is split into two projects, why the first reply asks about the reader, how a reader’s situation differs from the research scope, what approval means, how the research keeps the evidence that carries the answer, and what the delivered files promise. It is explanatory only. The two protocols are the contract the models follow; this guide cannot override them. How to install and use the projects is on the engine page.

1. Two projects, one contract

Deciding what to investigate and investigating it are different jobs with different risks. While the question is being agreed, the risk is that early findings shape the scope: a model that has already read three studies tends to draft a brief around them. While the research runs, the risk is the opposite: that inconvenient findings quietly narrow the question.

The engine separates the two jobs into two projects joined by one file:

  • The generation project may run a handful of searches to learn a field’s vocabulary or to check that an interpretation is coherent, but it does not appraise sources or report findings. It ends when you approve a brief and it issues the RB. It never researches.
  • The execution project never interviews you, approves anything or edits the RB. It treats the RB as the scope: contrary findings are reported, not used to change the question. A change of questions, corpus, mandatory cases or exclusions needs a new RB from the generation project.

The RB is the only thing passed between them. That makes the agreement inspectable: anyone holding the RB can see what the research was meant to answer, without the conversation that produced it. It also makes the research portable: the RB can be run in another app or by another model.

Inside the execution project, one assistant does everything in one continuous conversation: research, writing, rendering, checking and delivery. It does not hand work to other agents or models. This keeps one working record of what was read and why, and avoids losing evidence at handoffs between stages.

2. Asking about the reader first

A report is written for someone and for a use. The same evidence on screen time serves a parent setting family rules differently from a researcher checking a meta-analysis. Before 1.7b.10 the brief asked about the reader in passing, next to the format, and the model usually fell back to a generic reader.

2.1 A dedicated first turn

On a new topic, the first reply is a short message of questions and nothing else: no brief, no scope, no findings. It asks only about points that are still unknown and would change the report:

  1. the reader’s starting point: what they already know of the subject and its vocabulary;
  2. the use: what the report should let them do (decide, understand, explain to others, check or cite a claim);
  3. the readers: the person asking alone, or others, and who they are;
  4. the situation, only where it changes which evidence applies (a country, an age group, a setting).

Models can recognise that a request is ambiguous and still answer it directly instead of asking; clarifying an ambiguous information need is a known weak point of language models. [1] [2] A question that is optional inside a longer reply is easy to skip, so the engine makes the questions the whole first turn and requires the model to stop after them.

The turn is skipped when the request already answers every point that matters, when you waive it, when you continue an earlier topic in the same conversation, or when you revise an existing brief or RB.

2.2 Few, open questions, each with a default

Every question costs the reader attention. In a randomised study of AI-assisted interviews, follow-up probing produced more specific answers, but about doubled the length of the conversation and shifted the respondents’ experience slightly for the worse. [3] The engine therefore asks at most four questions, only those whose answer would change the report, in plain words. The number four is a design choice: the evidence shows that questions have a cost, not where the optimum lies.

Each question states the assumption the model will use if it stays unanswered, and one word, “skip”, accepts them all. You can answer some, all or none. The assumptions reach the RB as assumptions, so a later reader can tell what you said from what was assumed.

The questions are open. Offered answers shape answers: the range and the order of response options change what people report. [4] [5] A list such as “decide / understand / cite” would steer the reader toward the listed uses, so the protocol forbids presenting the uses, or any other list, as options to pick from.

2.3 Answers are not approval

A reply to the reader questions leads to a brief, never to an RB, even if it says “approved”: you have not seen a brief yet. The brief then shows the reader lines in plain words, marks assumed values, and says that they will be recorded in the RB.

3. Reader context is not scope

The situation answer needs care, because it can do two different things.

Most often it only changes which evidence is most applicable. A parent of a six-year-old in France asking about screen time still benefits from evidence on other ages and countries; the report should say where evidence for that age is thin and what French guidance says. The engine records this as reader context in RB section 8. The execution project uses it to decide which evidence is discussed as most applicable and where limits matter; it does not narrow the search or remove evidence.

Sometimes the situation should restrict the research: “only studies from France”, “only children under ten”. That is a scope decision. The engine shows it as a proposed boundary in the brief, and it becomes scope only when you approve it.

The recorded cases show why the distinction is written down. In three runs, a situation answer drifted into the scope (an age band of 5 to 7, the exclusion of under-25s) instead of staying reader context. The protocol says it should not; the cases show that models still do it, so the brief is the place to catch it before approval.

Personal details are recorded only as far as they change the report, impersonally and without names (“a parent of a child of about six”). The RB is a file you may share, and the report should be readable by anyone.

4. Truthful approval, preserved versions

4.1 Approval covers what you saw

Each proposed brief has an identifier (BR-001, then BR-002 for a revision). Approval refers to one brief, and the RB records which one was approved and on what basis. The RB may add headings, identifiers and faithful operational detail; it may not add a research question, a mandatory comparison, an evidence restriction or a deliverable that you did not see.

A reply that changes the scope is applied directly only when it is unambiguous and can be written into the RB word for word. Anything that would need interpretation is shown again as a new brief, and needs one more confirmation.

If you waive approval (“skip the brief”), the RB is issued with the status assumptions-not-explicitly-approved. A waiver allows the work to proceed; it never becomes a recorded approval.

4.2 Issued files do not change

An issued RB is kept byte for byte. A scope revision is a new RB file with its own version and a pointer to the one it supersedes. A corrected report is a new report version (topic.v1.1.md with matching reading copies). Scope versions and report versions are counted separately, because a corrected figure in a report does not change what the research was meant to answer.

5. Research the questions, keep the evidence

5.1 Investigate the questions, not the document

The execution project works from the RB’s questions. Each one closes with a supported answer, an investigated inconclusive result, or a disclosed evidence gap, after consequential counter-evidence has been considered. A failed retrieval is recorded as an access limit, never as proof that evidence does not exist. Normal closure also needs a reason why further searching within the scope would not add materially useful evidence; research stopped by a limit is labelled incomplete, with the unfinished questions named.

Evidence that drives a conclusion is appraised in context: what was tested, on whom or on what system, against what comparison, with what outcome, magnitude and uncertainty, and how far it applies to the reader’s setting. A citation being present is not the same as the cited source supporting the claim; the two are checked separately. [6]

5.2 Adapt the explanation, not the evidence

Writing for a reader invites simplification, and simplification can quietly drop the estimate, the denominator or the qualification that carries an answer. The execution protocol keeps one working record of decisive estimates, assumptions, conflicts and inspected source locations, and writes the report from it. Adapting to the reader changes how much is explained, in what order and with what vocabulary; it does not change which consequential evidence survives. The purpose decides what the reader meets first (a decision, an explanation for others, a claim to check and cite); the reader context decides which evidence is discussed as most applicable.

5.3 Reconcile once, then deliver

Before delivery, the claims that drive conclusions are rechecked against the inspected source locations, the questions are checked for coverage, and the delivered files are checked against each other. The reporting borrows items from scoping-review guidance (objectives, eligibility, sources, search, limitations) without claiming compliance with it. [7]

6. What you receive

File What it is
topic.html or topic.pdf The reading copy: self-contained HTML by default, PDF when the RB asks for one. Numbered citations link to an anchored bibliography with inspectable source locations.
topic.md The same content in Markdown, labelled as source for tools, not as the reading copy.
topic.rb.md The unmodified RB the research followed.
topic-research-package.zip All public files with their figures, when the report has any.

The body length is measured in a fixed A4 reference layout (11-point text, 25 mm margins). When that render is not possible in the app, the delivery says “Substantive body page count: unverified” instead of claiming compliance.

Every delivery states two separate outcomes: whether the research is complete or constrained-incomplete, and whether the delivery is complete or partial (a missing format, an exceeded limit, a substitute reading copy). It also lists which checks were performed and which were not.

7. Apps differ

The same instructions meet different hosts. Before relying on web access, file creation, code, PDF production or visual inspection, each project checks what the app actually provides and names a fallback for what it lacks: an HTML reading copy when PDF fails, inline substitutes when files cannot be created, an explicit “not performed” for checks the app cannot do. The execution project rechecks its own app; capabilities do not carry over from the generation project.

The recorded cases add what the protocol cannot control:

  • On lighter settings (ChatGPT Medium and Instant, Claude Haiku at Low) a simple-looking question was answered directly, without the engine.
  • Claude Fable 5.1 at Max judged the project instructions a prompt injection, because the app delivered them inside a tool result.
  • Mistral Vibe turns questions into multiple-choice popups, which works against open questions: type your own answer in the free field. Vibe cannot render PDFs, so ask for HTML reading copies.

8. Limitations

Project instructions are a request to the model; the app and the model decide whether to follow them. In the recorded cases, five of fifteen runs answered directly instead.

The checks are done by the same assistant that did the research, and are reported as performed or not performed. They are not independent verification of the findings.

The October 2026 benchmark measured engine 1.6. Version 1.7b.10 has not been benchmarked: its reader questions and its execution project are shown by the recorded cases, not measured. The sources cited here are background for the design choices, not experiments validating this engine.

Glossary

Term Meaning in this guide
Brief The proposal shown for approval before an RB exists, identified as BR-001, BR-002, and so on.
Evidence gap Adequate support was not found in the stated search; not proof that support does not exist.
Purpose/use What the reader will do with the report; it decides what the report leads with.
Reader context The reader’s situation where it makes some evidence more applicable; it shapes emphasis, not scope.
Reading copy The HTML or PDF meant to be read; the Markdown is source for tools.
Reference layout The fixed A4 rendering used to measure the body length.
Research Boundaries (RB) The standalone file defining the agreed research and its deliverable.
Waiver Your instruction to proceed without approving a brief; the RB then records assumptions, not approval.

 Summary
 The question. LLM assistants sometimes get facts wrong, or contradict themselves, as inputs
 and conversations grow longer or when they draw on earlier sessions. This review asks how far
 such failures relate to three things:
 • context length within a single input;
 • conversation length within a session;
 • cross-session memory, meaning the systems that store, retrieve and update information
    between sessions.
 It also asks whether a given failure can be attributed to one mechanism rather than another.
 The approved boundaries treat three premises as claims to test, not assumptions: that length
 degrades reliability, that the two mechanisms are empirically separable, and that effective
 context is smaller than the advertised window.
 What the controlled evidence shows.
 1. Within a single input, longer context does degrade reliability, even when the needed
 evidence is held fixed.
 • Two padding designs show this directly. Levy et al. (2024) padded inputs with duplicated,
   task-similar or unrelated text. Du et al. (2025) padded with whitespace, or with
   attention-masked tokens after confirming that the model could recite the evidence. Both found
   accuracy falling as length grew.
 • The effect is moderate to large in open models of 70B parameters or smaller, at 30K tokens or
   fewer. It is smaller, but not absent, in closed frontier models.
 • Distractors that resemble the target, and low lexical overlap between question and evidence,
   make the decline much steeper (NoLiMa; Context Rot; Jin et al. 2025).
 • Position effects ("lost in the middle") are real but conditional. They are clearest at short
   absolute lengths and when the input fills less than about half the window. Beyond that, they
   shift toward a simple recency gradient (Veseli et al. 2025).
 2. Effective context is smaller than the advertised window on almost every non-trivial
 test, but the size of the gap depends on how "effective" is defined.
 • RULER defines it with an absolute threshold. NoLiMa defines it relative to each model's own
    short-context score. BABILong expresses it as a share of the window.
 • These definitions give numbers that are not interchangeable: from "more than 128K" to "2K or
    less" for the same generation of models.
 • Single-needle retrieval tests saturate near 100% and say little about factual reliability on
    realistic tasks (RULER; HELMET; the Gemini 1.5 report itself).
 3. Within a session, multi-turn interaction causes large drops, but mostly not because
 of turn count or token count as such.
 • When a request is revealed piece by piece over several turns, accuracy falls by about 35–39%
   on average. Giving the same pieces in one message keeps about 95% of single-turn
   performance (Laban et al. 2025).
 • Designs that control what the model sees point to four causes:
   – underspecification: the model answers before it has the whole request;
   – conditioning on its own earlier mistakes: self-generated errors carried forward in the
     history (MT-Eval; Sinha et al.);
   – interference from superseded facts: this persists even at fixed total length (PI-LLM);
   – social pressure in user turns: for example, giving way when challenged (FlipFlop).
 • Pure length still contributes a smaller residual decline. Neutral filler turns can be harmless.

 4. Cross-session memory fails in ways that are distinct from long-context failures. The
 best-supported memory failure modes are:
 • updating and resolving contradictions;
 • temporal reasoning across sessions;

 •   aggregating facts spread over several sessions;
 •   answering false-premise questions instead of abstaining;
 •   write-stage loss (facts never stored, or stored with the wrong date);
 •   applying a stored fact after it has stopped being true.
 When memory pipelines are split into stages, retrieval failures dominate in some studies (Yuan
 et al. 2026). In others, failures split roughly evenly between retrieval and reading
 (LongMemEval).
 5. The two mechanisms can be separated in controlled experiments, but they are
 nested rather than parallel. A retrieved memory is itself placed into a context that the model
 must read. Oracle-retrieval conditions, length-matched controls, stage-wise decompositions and
 route substitution (retrieval versus long context, per query) each isolate one factor. None
 isolates all of them.
 What this means for attribution.
 • Attribution holds at the population level: under a named controlled contrast, on a stated
     benchmark.
 • It rarely holds for a single failure in a deployed assistant. The reasons:
   – the pipelines are undocumented;
   – outputs vary from run to run;
   – judge and annotation noise is often as large as the effects being attributed;
   – the model's own explanations are not valid evidence of which mechanism failed.

 Product reports. Vendor documentation and user reports are anecdotal evidence, used here
 only as illustrations.
 • Vendors' own documentation concedes the main limits:
   – memory is selective;
   – deleting a memory does not take effect immediately;
   – provenance displays are incomplete;
   – long chats are summarised as they near the window limit;
   – effective context is smaller than the nominal window.
 • User reports describe failures that resemble the controlled failure modes. They cannot
     establish which mechanism caused any particular failure.

 Verdict on the three premises. (1) Longer context and longer conversations degrade factual
 reliability: supported, with boundary conditions. The effect is task- and model-dependent, and in
 multi-turn settings it is driven more by interaction structure than by length. (2) The two mechanisms
 are empirically separable: supported in controlled settings, not for individual deployed failures. (3)
 Effective utilisation is smaller than the advertised window: supported on every non-trivial benchmark,
 though its magnitude depends on the definition used.



 1. Scope and method
 1.1 What was reviewed
 This is a structured narrative evidence review. It is not systematic, so it makes no claim to have
 found every relevant study.
 In scope: text-based LLMs and LLM assistant systems, including systems with retrieval-based
 or other cross-session memory modules; work from 2020 to the date of search (3 November
 2026); and three topics: processing of long inputs within a session, multi-turn interaction within
 a session (what "conversation length" means here), and cross-session memory (how information
 is stored, retrieved, updated and reasoned about in time).
 Mitigation techniques (retrieval, summarisation, compaction) appear only where they help
 explain or separate the mechanisms. They are not assessed or recommended.

 Out of scope: multimodal-only work; factual errors that come from training-data knowledge
 and are unrelated to context or memory; and any comparison or ranking of commercial
 products.

 1.2 Search and appraisal
 Evidence was gathered in five parallel sweeps: single long inputs; multi-turn interaction within
 one session; cross-session memory; evaluation designs that separate the mechanisms and the
 limits of attribution; and reports about commercial products. Each sweep searched arXiv, the
 ACL Anthology, OpenReview and conference proceedings, and deliberately looked for negative
 results, boundary conditions and competing explanations. For each study that drives a
 conclusion, the review recorded design, models and versions, context lengths, comparators and
 controls, metrics, uncertainty reporting, confounds, and level of access (condensed in Section
 10). Numbers were checked against primary sources where accessible; values available only
 from figures or secondary reports are marked as such.

 1.3 Labels used in this report
 Publication status. Each study is labelled peer-reviewed (with venue), preprint, technical
 report or vendor blog. Several 2025–2026 papers were available only as preprints. Their
 findings are used but flagged, and they do not carry a conclusion on their own.
 Strength of conclusion.

Label             Meaning
Strong            Several independent studies with controlled designs agree.
Moderate          Consistent controlled evidence, but from few groups, few models or synthetic tasks only.
Limited           A single study, preprint-only support, abstract-only access, or a substantial design confound.

 Evidence classes.
 • Controlled evaluations are the primary evidence.
 • Product reports (vendor documentation, vendor announcements, journalism, forum posts)
    form a separate anecdotal class. Section 8 covers them. They are never treated as evidence
    that a specific mechanism caused a failure.

 1.4 The two mechanisms, and where the line between them blurs
 Mechanism (a): within-session context processing. Everything the model needs is in its
 context window. The failure is in how the model uses that context.
 Mechanism (b): cross-session memory. Information from earlier sessions has to be written
 to a store, indexed, retrieved, inserted into the current context, and kept up to date. The failure
 can happen at any of these steps.
 These are not mutually exclusive.
 • A retrieved memory ends up inside a context window. So a memory failure can be a reading
    failure, mechanism (a) operating inside (b).
 • Systems that summarise or "compact" a long conversation within one session use the same
    lossy operations as memory systems. They do so without crossing a session boundary.
 Following the approved boundaries, such cases are assigned according to how the study or
 system describes its own mechanism. Where that assignment is ambiguous, the report says so.


 2. Measuring "effective context"
 No definition of effective context utilisation has been agreed. The review therefore reports each
 source's definition instead of imposing one. This matters because the definitions produce
 numbers that cannot be compared with each other.

 3. Q1a: Reliability within a single long context
 Intuition. A model reading a long input has to do two things. First it must find the information
 that matters. Then it must use that information correctly, with everything else still in view. A
 failure could come from either step. It could also come from the input simply being long, even
 when nothing in it is misleading. The studies below try to pull these three possibilities apart.

 3.1 Length itself, with the evidence held fixed
 Design. The cleanest designs keep the task and its evidence identical and change only how
 much extra material surrounds it.
 Levy, Jacoby and Goldberg (ACL 2024): FLenQA
 • Setup:
   – Three true/false reasoning tasks, 100 base instances each.
   – Two key paragraphs were padded to about 250–3,000 tokens with one of three kinds of
     filler:
     · duplicate copies of the key paragraphs, which adds almost no new information;
     · paragraphs from other instances of the same task;
     · unrelated BookCorpus text.
   – Key paragraphs were placed at the start, middle or end, or dispersed.
 • Models: GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Gemini Pro, Mistral Medium and Mixtral 8x7B, with and without
   chain-of-thought (CoT).
 • Result:
   – Mean accuracy fell from 0.92 at about 250 tokens to 0.68 at about 3,000 tokens. That is far
     below any of these models' limits.
   – The fall appeared even with duplicate padding.
   – Unrelated text usually hurt more than similar text.
   – Dispersed evidence was worst. Evidence at the end was generally best.
   – CoT added a roughly constant gain but did not close the gap (GPT-4 was the exception).
   – As inputs grew, models refused more often, drifted toward answering "False", and their
     reasoning covered fewer of the relevant facts.
 • Limitations: Lengths are short by current standards. Binary labels mean the drift toward
   "False" interacts with the accuracy measure. The closed models were tested at undisclosed
   dates.

 Du et al. (Findings of EMNLP 2025): "Context Length Alone Hurts…"
 • Setup: This design goes further. Each prompt has evidence, then distraction, then the
     question.
     – Retrieval is measured separately by asking the model to recite the evidence and question
       exactly. Accuracy can then be analysed only on items where retrieval succeeded.
     – Three kinds of distraction:
       · Paul Graham essays;
       · whitespace;
       · attention-masked tokens, which advance positional indices but carry no visible content.
 •   Models: Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, GPT-4o, a Claude 3.x model and Gemini 2.0.
 •   Tasks: variable summation, GSM8K, MMLU and HumanEval.
 •   Result:
     – Performance fell by between 13.9% and 85% within the models' claimed windows, even
       when retrieval was perfect.
     – With whitespace padding, Llama lost 48 points on variable summation.
     – With masked padding, the open models lost at least 7.9% at 30K tokens.
     – Closed models degraded less (GPT-4o lost about 7 points on GSM8K).
 •   Limitations:
     – All experiments were run once, and per-condition sample sizes are not clearly reported.
     – Masking removes content but keeps position distance, so "length" here partly means
       positional distance.
     – Masking is possible only for open-weight models.

 Conclusion 3.1 (Moderate). Input length degrades accuracy even when the relevant evidence is
 fixed and can be shown to have been retrieved. The effect is well replicated in open models of 70B or
 less at 30K tokens or less. It is smaller in closed frontier models, and it has not been measured with
 this level of control at the scale of modern windows (hundreds of thousands of tokens).



 3.2 Position of the relevant information
 Liu et al., "Lost in the Middle" (TACL 2024)
 • Setup: The position of a gold passage was moved among 10–30 retrieved distractors, about
     1.5K–4.4K tokens. There was also a synthetic key–value retrieval task.
 • Design strength: Two reference points frame the results:
   – Closed-book: no documents at all.
   – Oracle: the gold document only.
 • Result: For GPT-3.5-Turbo with 20 documents:
   – 75.8% with the gold passage first;
   – 53.8% at the worst middle position;
   – 56.1% closed-book;
   – 88.3% oracle.

 Burying the evidence in the middle therefore did worse than giving no context at all. The curve
 is U-shaped, with primacy and recency advantages.
 Later work makes this effect conditional rather than universal.
 • Veseli et al. (2025, preprint) varied input length as a fraction of each model's window, from
   0.06 to 1.0, across six open models.
   – The U-shape was strongest when inputs filled up to about 50% of the window.
   – Beyond that, the primacy advantage disappeared and only recency remained.
   – Once the analysis was restricted to items where retrieval succeeded, the positional bias in
      reasoning largely flattened.
   – The authors suggest this reconciles a strong mid-context effect in short-input studies with
      its weakness in benchmarks above 100K tokens.
 • Chroma's Context Rot report (2025, vendor technical report): across 18 models, needle
   position had no notable effect, while length and distractors did.

 Both suggest the effect comes from how models are trained and how attention is biased, not

 Conclusion 3.2 (Strong for existence; Moderate for scope). Position effects are robust at short
 lengths and partial window fill. At high window fill, primacy weakens and recency dominates. The bias
 is reduced by targeted training and calibration. A mid-context failure in one model, at one length, does
 not predict the same failure elsewhere.



 3.3 Distractors, similarity and literal matching
 Intuition. A long context is rarely just noise. It contains material that looks relevant but is not.
 Several designs show that this resemblance, more than raw length, decides how badly models
 fail.
 Short-context baseline: Shi et al. (ICML 2023, GSM-IC)
 • Adding a single irrelevant sentence to easy GSM8K problems reduced the share of base
     problems solved under every distractor variant to below 30%.
 • The best "macro accuracy" (solved under all variants) was about 45%.
 • Distractors on the same topic, or containing numbers in the problem's range, were the most
     damaging.
 Retrieval-augmented settings: Jin et al. (ICLR 2025)
 • As more passages were retrieved, output quality first rose and then fell, even though retrieval
     recall kept increasing.
 • The main cause was "hard negatives": passages that score as relevant but do not contain the
     answer.
 • Higher retriever recall therefore does not guarantee higher accuracy.

 Literal matching: NoLiMa (ICML 2025)
 • Setup: Needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH) tests where the needle and question share minimal
   vocabulary. Linking them requires one or two steps of association. 13 models with windows of
   128K or more.
 • Result:
   – At 32K, 11 of the 13 models fell below 50% of their own short-context score.
   – GPT-4o went from 99.3% to 69.7%.
   – Supplying literal cues as multiple-choice options raised Llama-3.3-70B's 2-hop accuracy at
     32K from 25.9% to 87.2%. This shows that word overlap is what makes standard NIAH
     easy.
   – Reasoning models also fell below half their base score on the hard subset.

 Similarity and coherence: Context Rot (Chroma, 2025)
 •   Lower similarity between needle and question made performance fall faster with length.
 •   One distractor hurt, and four hurt more.
 •   Shuffled haystacks scored better than coherent ones in all 18 models.
 •   Model families differed in failure style: some abstained more, others hallucinated more.
 •   Chroma sells retrieval infrastructure, which should be kept in mind.
 2026 frontier models: PredicateLongBench (Jain & Velingker 2026, preprint)
 • At a fixed 128K tokens, enlarging the search space dropped one frontier model's accuracy
     from 92% to 10%.

 Conclusion 3.3 (Strong). Distractors that resemble the target, and low lexical overlap between
 question and evidence, are major causes of failure inside long contexts. Their effect grows with length.
 Benchmarks that let a model find evidence by literal matching overstate reliability.



 3.4 What saturated benchmarks miss
 Vanilla NIAH no longer discriminates between models.
 • Vendor reports cite near-perfect single-needle recall at very large windows. For example, the
    Gemini 1.5 report gives above 99.7% up to 1M tokens.
 • The same report shows much lower scores on harder variants:
   – about 70% for 100 needles at 128K;
   – a little above 60% at 1M.
 • The report itself calls single-needle NIAH a simple, prompt-sensitive test.

 Prompt sensitivity is large.
 • Anthropic reported that one model's single-needle accuracy rose from 27% to 98% after
    adding a short prefill to the response.
 • Machlab and Battle (2024, preprint) found that changing only the needle and question moved
    one model between 100% and 68.2%.
 • Needles that conflict with what the model already knows did worst, because the model fell
    back on its prior knowledge.
 Synthetic scores do not track realistic tasks well. HELMET (ICLR 2025)
 • Tested 59 models across seven application categories.
 • Across 35 instruction-tuned models at 128K, the Spearman correlation between synthetic
    recall scores and downstream tasks never averaged above 0.8.
 • Retrieval-augmented QA tasks were the best single proxy.

 Length is often confounded with difficulty in realistic benchmarks. LongBench v2 (ACL
 2025)
 • Scores are not monotone in length. For one leading model, "long" items scored higher than
    "medium" ones.
 • The length buckets contain different documents and different questions, so length is
    confounded with how hard the question is. Human expert scores were also non-monotone
    (47.2 / 59.1 / 53.7).

 Conclusion 3.4 (Strong). Near-perfect single-needle recall is not evidence of reliable fact use at that
 length. Tasks that need aggregation, tracking, multi-hop linking or resistance to decoys show effective
 lengths well below advertised windows (RULER; SYNC; Michelangelo; BABILong). Realistic benchmarks
 need length-matched designs before a length effect can be read from them.



 3.5 Proposed internal mechanisms
 Several accounts converge. They are mostly tested on open models of 7–70B parameters.
 Architectural position bias. Wu et al. (ICML 2025) analyse attention masks theoretically.
 • Causal masking pushes deeper layers toward early tokens.
 • Relative positional encodings such as RoPE add distance decay.
 • The interaction between the two can produce primacy and attention sinks.

 Under-trained long distances. An et al. (ICLR 2025) argue that long relative positions are
 rare during training.

 • A training-free shift of position indices (STRING) improved RULER scores by about 15 points
    for one 70B model.
 • This is an interventional result. It supports the account but does not exclude alternatives.

 "Knows but doesn't tell." Lu et al. (Findings of EMNLP 2024) trained layer-wise linear
 probes.
 • The probes located mid-context targets more accurately than the models' generated answers
    did.
 • Hsieh C.-Y. et al. found that calibrated attention ranks the gold document far better than raw
    attention.
 Together these suggest that, in these models, information is often encoded but not used.
 Copying circuits. Wu W. et al. (ICLR 2025) identified sparse "retrieval heads", under 5% of
 heads.
 • Pruning them caused retrieval failures and hallucination. Pruning random heads did not.
 • This gives a causal, component-level signature for "failed to copy from context" in open
    models.

 Conclusion 3.5 (Moderate for open models; inferred only for closed models). Position bias,
 under-training at long distances, and failure to use encoded information are all supported by
 interventional evidence in open models. Whether the same mechanisms explain failures in closed
 frontier models has not been shown.



 3.6 Boundary conditions and counterevidence
 Long context does not always lose to retrieval. With enough compute, long-context reading beat
 retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) on average for three proprietary models (Li Z. et al.,
 EMNLP 2024 Industry). LaRA (ICML 2025) found the winner depends on conditions: long
 context won at 32K, for strong models and on comparison tasks; RAG won at 128K, for weaker
 models and at detecting that the answer is absent. Closed models degraded far less than open
 ones in Du et al., and in SYNC (EMNLP 2025) one closed model held 93–96% on a graph task
 where an open 70B model fell from 98.7% to 11.3% between 32K and 128K. Degradation is not
 monotone everywhere: Michelangelo (Google DeepMind preprint) found a sharp early drop,
 often by 32K, after which some models flattened up to 1M tokens. Most controlled evidence
 covers lengths of 32K or less, open models from 2023–2025, and single runs without confidence
 intervals; frontier-model evidence from 2026 is sparse and mostly preprint.


 4. Q1b: Multi-turn accumulation within a session
 Intuition. A long conversation differs from a long document in two ways:
 • The information arrives in pieces, and the model often answers before it has all of them.
 • Much of the context was written by the model itself, so earlier mistakes become part of what it
    later reads.
 Either difference, rather than length as such, could explain degradation.

    –More than 200,000 conversations across 15 models from eight families, with 10 simulations
     per model and condition.

 • An intent-rewriting mediator recovered roughly 55–62% of the gap for frontier models (Liu et

 several turns causes large drops and much greater run-to-run variability. Delivering the same content
 in one turn does not. The best-supported cause is underspecification combined with premature
 commitment, not turn count or token count.



 4.2 Conditioning on self-generated errors
 Three designs isolate what the model's own earlier turns contribute.
 Gold-history swap: MT-Eval (Kwan et al., EMNLP 2024)
 • In recollection tasks, replacing the model's earlier answers with correct ones raised judge
    scores substantially. For example, Mixtral went from 3.21 to 7.11 on a 10-point scale.
 • In an audit of 160 low-scoring responses, about 48% had been misled by errors that
    accumulated earlier in the conversation.
 Injected error rates: Sinha et al. (2025/2026, preprint)

[Original PDF page 12 of 29]
 • A synthetic task: look up values and keep a running sum, with the plan and the knowledge
    supplied in the prompt.
 • Artificial assistant histories were injected with controlled error rates.
 • With an error-free history, accuracy at turn 100 was still below turn 1. That residual is the
    pure long-context component.
 • Raising the injected error rate lowered accuracy further. That is the self-conditioning
    component.
 • Scaling to larger models did not remove self-conditioning. The "thinking" models tested were
    largely immune.
 Length-matched neutralisation: Li et al. (2026, preprint)
 • Replayed the SHARDED conversations, replacing earlier assistant turns with "Acknowledged."
 • A length-matched version padded the placeholder to the original turn's length.
 • Short and length-matched neutralisation produced the same effect, with a difference of
    −0.001 (95% CI −0.039 to 0.035). Shortening the context is therefore not what drives it.
 • The effect was mixed:
   – neutralisation rescued about 51% of failures but broke about 15% of successes;
   – the net macro effect was small (+0.027);
   – some models and tasks got worse.

 Extra turns without new information. Huang et al. (ICLR 2024) show that turns adding no
 new information can hurt. Unprompted self-correction lowered GPT-3.5's CommonSenseQA
 accuracy from 75.8% to about 40% over two rounds.

 Conclusion 4.2 (Moderate). The content of the model's own earlier turns is a separate,
 model-dependent cause of multi-turn degradation, distinct from length. It mostly harms but sometimes
 helps. Three independent designs agree on its existence. They disagree on its average size.



 4.3 Interference when facts are updated within a session
 PI-LLM (Wang & Sun 2025, preprint)
 • Setup:
   – A stream of key–value updates is presented, and the model must report the latest value for
     each key.
    –The answer is always the most recent update, so finding it is trivial.
    –Updates per key were varied from 3 to 400.
 • Result:
   – Accuracy fell roughly log-linearly toward zero as the number of updates grew.
   – Most errors were superseded values: the model reported an older value instead of the
     latest one.
   – In a length-controlled experiment, total input length was held fixed while the number of
     tracked keys varied. The decline remained.
   – Across 30 models, parameter count predicted resistance to this interference (p = 0.005).
     Context-window size did not (p = 0.886).
   – Chain-of-thought did not help.
   – Natural-language instructions to "forget" helped by less than 10 points.

 This bears directly on conversations in which a user corrects a fact. Of all within-session
 findings, it maps most closely onto the update failures seen in cross-session memory (Section 5).

 Conclusion 4.3 (Moderate; single preprint, but with a length control). Interference from
 superseded values degrades in-context fact tracking independently of total length. It may be a shared
 factor behind update failures in both mechanisms.

 4.4 Social pressure in user turns
 In FlipFlop (Laban et al. 2023/2024, preprint; 67,640 two-turn experiments, 10 models, 7 tasks),
 a single user challenge made models change their answer about 46% of the time and cut
 accuracy by about 17%. SYCON-Bench (Hong et al., preprint) found that under five turns of
 repeated pushback, larger models gave way less often and alignment tuning was associated
 with giving way more; only one of its three scenarios concerns factual premises. This is a
 separate cause that a single turn can trigger, independent of length, but it is easily mistaken for
 "the conversation got long."

 4.5 Compaction and summarisation within a session: a boundary case
 Assistants that summarise earlier turns as a chat nears the window limit use a lossy operation
 that also appears in memory systems. The evidence on this is thin.
 Recursive summarisation (Wang Q. et al., Neurocomputing)
 • A manual audit of 100 summarised memories found:
   – fabricated facts in 2.7%;
   – wrong relations in 3.2%;
   – missing details in 3.9%.
 • The setting is cross-session (MSC), but the same error types arise in compaction within a
    session.
 CONPACT (Wang Z. et al. 2026, preprint; abstract only)
 • Compactors retained about 17% of injected side constraints on average.
 • In one agent test, compaction preceded unapproved deletions in 30 of 100 sessions, against 0
    before compaction.
 • These are constraints, not facts.

 Under the approved boundaries, such systems are assigned according to how they describe
 themselves. A compacting assistant that keeps the full history available for reference is
 within-session. A failure caused by its summary has the character of a memory-write failure, so
 the distinction is a matter of definition rather than of evidence.

 4.6 Counterevidence: length alone is not always harmful
 BoolQ with filler turns (Neergaard et al. 2026, preprint)
 • Setup:
   – The target question was placed after 1–21 filler turns.
   – Three small open models were tested.
 • Result:
   – Neutral filler turns were mostly harmless or slightly beneficial.
   – Harm appeared only when misleading content was combined with length.

 Drift over turns (Dongre et al. 2025, workshop)
 • Behavioural drift settled at bounded equilibria rather than growing without limit.

 Turn counts in the sharding study (Laban et al.)
 • Degradation did not keep growing as the number of shards increased.

 Overall. No verified study fully crosses turn count, token length, level of specification, and
 correct versus self-generated history. Elements of that design exist across Laban et al., Sinha et
 al., Li et al. (2026) and Neergaard et al., but no single study combines them.

 Conclusion 4.6 (Moderate). "Conversation length" is not a single cause. The large multi-turn effects
 come from how information is disclosed and from the model's own history. Pure length adds a smaller
 residual. Neutral turns can be harmless, and misleading turns are not.

 5. Q2: Failures of cross-session memory
 Intuition. A memory system can go wrong at four points:
 • Write: it may never store a fact, or store it wrongly. This includes stamping it with the date it
    was mentioned rather than the date it happened.
 • Retrieve: it may fail to bring the fact back when it is needed.
 • Read: it may bring the fact back and the model may still misuse it.
 • Update: it may keep using a fact after that fact has stopped being true.

 Each step can fail on its own, and the error a user sees looks the same whichever step caused it.

 5.4 Do memory systems beat simply reading the whole history?
 This question decides whether a benchmark exercises mechanism (b) at all. The evidence is
 contested.
 LoCoMo histories fit inside modern windows, and full-context baselines win there.
 • A vendor's own paper (Chhikara et al. 2025, Mem0) found full context had the highest judged
   accuracy (J = 72.90). All memory systems tested scored lower. The headline gains it claimed
   were in latency and token cost.
 • An independent audit of the LoCoMo answer key (2026) reported:
   – 6.4% of gold answers are wrong, capping attainable accuracy near 93.6%;
   – the LLM judge accepted 62.81% of answers that were deliberately wrong but vague;
   – a full-context baseline with a chain-of-thought prompt scored about 92.6%.
 • Rival vendors disputed one another's harnesses. Disputed choices included how speaker roles
   were assigned and where timestamps were placed, and one vendor could not reproduce
   another's setup. Their figures for the same system differ by about 9 points.
 On LongMemEval-S (~115K tokens) the evidence is mixed.
 • A vendor-authored evaluation (Zep) reported 71.2% against 60.2% for full context with
   GPT-4o, while cutting context from about 115K to 1.6K tokens. It also lost accuracy on facts
   the assistant itself had stated.
 • Single-factor re-analyses found parity:

 supported: update, temporal reasoning, aggregation, abstention, write-stage loss and over-application.
 Their relative size against long-context failures depends on whether the history fits in the window,
 which retriever and reader are used, and how judging is done. Leaderboard-style comparisons of
 memory systems are not reliable evidence. Many are vendor-run, single-run, judged by lenient LLM
 judges, and disputed.

 Three points stand out.
 1. The oracle condition is the single most informative design for separating the two
 mechanisms. Its gap has a specific meaning, though. In LongMemEval, the oracle-versus-S
 gap measures the cost of finding and reading the evidence inside a long history. That is
 mechanism (a) together with retrieval in context. It does not measure whether a memory
 system would have stored the evidence. Only update-targeted, write-targeted and longitudinal
 designs reach the store itself, and they are the least mature group.
 2. Length controls and oracle conditions are rarely combined. Du et al. combine a
 recitation check, which confirms retrieval, with padding that adds no content.
 MemoryAgentBench combines conflict resolution with a short-versus-long check. These are the
 closest the literature comes to a full separation. Both still cover few models.
 3. Validity critiques limit what any one design can establish.
 • Literal matching inflates scores on needle tests (NoLiMa).
 • Synthetic scores correlate only moderately with realistic tasks (HELMET).
 • LLM judges can differ from human scores by 5–10 points while showing over 90% agreement,
    and they accept vague wrong answers (Thakur et al. 2024/2025; the LoCoMo audit).
 • Answer keys contain errors (LoCoMo).

 Effects smaller than about 5–10 judged points should be treated as unresolved unless the judge
 has been validated on the benchmark itself, as in LongMemEval at about 97% agreement and
 Yuan et al. at κ = 0.82.


 7. Q4: How far can a specific failure be attributed?
 7.1 What can be attributed, and at what level
 Attribution is defensible at the population level. That requires three things:
 • a named controlled contrast, such as oracle versus full history, padded versus unpadded, or
    CONCAT versus SHARDED;
 • a stated benchmark and set of models;
 • an effect larger than the measurement noise.

 Under those conditions the literature supports statements such as:
 • Holding evidence fixed, this model loses N points from added length.
 • About half of this system's errors on this benchmark occur after correct retrieval.
 • Revealing the request across turns, rather than the information content, accounts for this
    drop.
 For a single observed failure in a deployed assistant, attribution is rarely possible. The
 reasons are set out below.

 7.2 Limits on attribution
 1. The mechanisms are nested, not parallel.




Context, conversation length and cross-session memory — evidence review                                              18

[Original PDF page 19 of 29]
 • Retrieved memories are placed into a prompt. A cross-session error can therefore come from
   retrieval failing, or from the reader losing a memory that was correctly retrieved. The second
   case is mechanism (a) operating inside (b).
 • LongMemEval's gains from reading strategy under perfect retrieval show this interaction
   directly. So does RAGChecker's finding that higher retriever recall increases a generator's
   sensitivity to noise.
 • Because of this nesting, results from single-factor designs cannot simply be added up into a
   complete breakdown.
 2. Effects are non-monotone and interact.
 • Several studies find inverted-U curves as more is retrieved: Jin et al.; order-preserving RAG;
    the Databricks long-context RAG study.
 • LaRA finds interactions among model, length and task.
 • A cause identified in one configuration therefore does not carry over to another.

 3. Outputs vary from run to run.
 • Laban et al. found unreliability of about 30% in multi-turn settings even at temperature 0.
 • A single failure may simply be sampling variation. Attributing it requires repeated trials, and
    most benchmark papers report single runs.
 4. Measurement noise is comparable to the effects.
 • Judge–human gaps of 5–10 points; acceptance of vague wrong answers; about 6% of gold
    answers wrong; rankings that change with the judge (Spencer 2026).
 • Each of these can be as large as the difference being attributed.

 5. Deployed systems are opaque.
 • From the outside, nobody can see:
   – whether a memory was written;
   – what was retrieved;
   – how the context was assembled;
   – whether compaction occurred;
   – which model version, or which serving configuration, handled a turn.
 • Vendor documentation states that provenance displays are incomplete (Section 8).

 6. Model self-reports are not valid evidence.
 • Turpin et al. (NeurIPS 2023) showed that chain-of-thought explanations can leave out the
    factor that actually drove the answer.
 • An assistant saying "I couldn't find that in our earlier chats", or citing a memory, does not
    establish which mechanism failed.
 7. Benchmarks sometimes measure a different mechanism from the one they name.
 • When a "memory" benchmark fits within the context window, failures on it are largely failures
    of long-context reading (Section 5.4).
 • When a needle test can be solved by literal matching, success on it is not evidence of how the
    model uses context (NoLiMa).

 8. Q5: Controlled evidence compared with reports about
 commercial products
 How to read this section. Everything here belongs to the anecdotal evidence class. It is
 included to show how product reports relate to the controlled findings. It is not evidence that
 any mechanism caused any reported failure, and it does not compare or rank products. Vendors
 are named only so the sources can be traced.

 8.1 What vendor documentation concedes
 Public documentation of consumer memory features from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and
 Microsoft, collected in 2026, describes several limits without any external prompting:
 Selective storage and retrieval
 • Memory does not keep every detail of past chats.
 • Chat-history reference is not a fixed list of facts.
 • When memory is full, entries can be deprioritised by recency or frequency.

 Deletion lag
 • Deleted memories, or memories from deleted chats, can keep being referenced for days. One
    vendor documents up to seven days.
 • Turning memory off may stop it being applied without deleting what is stored.

 Incomplete provenance
 • Source displays may show only some of what influenced a reply.
 • A "saved info" label can sometimes be wrong.
 • One enterprise product documents that memory writes produce no audit-log entries.

 Compaction within a session
 • At least one product automatically summarises earlier messages as a chat nears its window
    limit, while keeping the full history available for reference.
 • What the summary keeps is not documented.

 Effective context below the nominal window
 • One vendor's engineering guidance names "context rot": recall and reasoning over details
    weaken as the window fills.
 • It describes context as a finite attention budget.
 • Read through secondary summaries; the primary page was not reachable.

 Version churn
 • Memory designs changed several times between 2024 and 2026 at each vendor. Examples:
   – adding reference to past chats;
   – moving from a periodically refreshed summary to topic-level memories;
   – a background "synthesis" process.
 • A product report is therefore a snapshot of one configuration.

 What is not documented is what attribution would need:

 8.4 Where the two evidence classes meet, and where they do not
 Alignment
 • Product reports and vendor documents describe the same categories of failure as the
   controlled literature:
   – effective context smaller than the window;
   – stale or unupdated facts;
   – lossy summarisation;
   – retrieval of the wrong item;
   – confident continuity without retrieval.
 • A controlled study, LongMemEval, found deployed memory pipelines losing 37–64% against
   offline reading on the same histories. That is consistent with users experiencing memory
   errors on short histories.
 Non-transfer from controlled evaluations to products
 • Controlled results come from API models with known inputs.
 • Products add steps that no controlled study observes:
   – undocumented pipelines for retrieval, summarisation and context assembly;
   – routing across models;
   – safety layers;
   – frequent version changes.
 • A benchmark score for the underlying model does not predict how a product behaves.

 Non-transfer from products to controlled conclusions
 • User reports are self-selected and of unknown scale.
 • Their symptoms can come from serving bugs or model swaps that look like context or memory
    failure.
 • They cannot test any mechanistic claim, and under the approved boundaries they are not used
    to do so.


 9. Answer to the primary question (Q0) and verdict on
 the premises
 Q0. How do context length, conversation length and cross-session memory retrieval
 each relate to factual reliability, and how far can failures be attributed to one
 mechanism?
 Context length.
 • Relationship: real, and partly independent of content. Holding evidence fixed and confirming
    retrieval, added length still costs accuracy.
 • The cost is amplified most by:
   – distractors that resemble the target;
   – low lexical overlap between question and evidence;



Context, conversation length and cross-session memory — evidence review                                               22

[Original PDF page 23 of 29]
   – evidence scattered through the context.
 • It is modulated by position, conditionally on how full the window is.
 • It is substantially reduced, but not removed, in closed frontier models.
 • Mechanisms are supported by interventional evidence in open models only.

 Conversation length.
 • Relationship: large degradations, driven mainly by:
   – how information is disclosed across turns (underspecification and premature commitment);
   – conditioning on the model's own earlier errors;
   – interference from superseded facts;
   – social pressure.
 • Raw token length adds a smaller residual decline.
 • Neutral turns can be harmless.
 • Turn count as such has not been cleanly isolated.

 Cross-session memory.
 • Relationship: failure modes that are its own:
   – write loss and mis-dating;
   – retrieval misses;
   – stale facts not updated;
   – stale memory applied after conditions change.
 • Two of these are hard for every architecture tested: temporal reasoning and multi-hop
    reasoning across sessions.
 • Where the history fits in the window, memory systems often do no better than reading the full
    history. On such benchmarks, "memory failure" is largely long-context failure.
 Attribution.
 • Controlled designs can separate the mechanisms at the population level. The main ones are
   oracle conditions, length-matched padding, stage decomposition, route substitution and
   manipulation of the model's own history.
 • They cannot do so for an individual failure in a deployed assistant.
 • The mechanisms are nested: retrieval feeds a long-context read. Separating them therefore
   always needs a combination of designs, not one contrast.

 11. Limitations of this review
 Not systematic.
 • This is a narrative review. Searches were broad but not exhaustive, and no formal risk-of-bias
    tool was applied.
 • Five parallel sweeps gathered the evidence. Overlaps between them were reconciled by hand.
    Where the sweeps reported different venues or figures for the same study, the review used
    the version checked against the venue page.
 Recency and status.
 • Several important 2025–2026 findings are unreviewed preprints:
   – self-conditioning (Sinha et al.);
   – length-matched neutralisation (Li et al.);
   – the stage factorial (Yuan et al.);
   – MemTrapBench;
   – window-fill position effects (Veseli et al.).
 • They are labelled as preprints, and no conclusion rests on one of them alone.
 • Some 2026 preprints name model versions that could not be independently checked.

 Access.
 • Some studies were read in abstract only: Jin et al.; BABILong at the level of per-model results;
    MemBench; PersonaMem; CONPACT.
 • Several long papers were read only in part.
 • Figures available only in charts are given as approximate.
 • Several vendor primary pages were unreachable and were read through secondary sources.
    These are marked in Section 8.
 Narrow evidence base.
 • Controlled evidence is concentrated on:
   – synthetic tasks;
   – simulated users and histories;
   – open models of 70B or less;
   – lengths of 32K or less, except for the memory benchmarks.
 • Few studies report confidence intervals or repeated runs.
 • Results for frontier closed models at windows of hundreds of thousands of tokens remain thin
    and mostly vendor-reported.
 Not covered: training-data knowledge. Factual errors that come from what the model
 learned in training are excluded by scope. Several studies note that models fall back on
 parametric knowledge when evidence in context is hard to use, for example Machlab & Battle
 and the RULER failure analysis. The excluded mechanism therefore interacts with the included
 ones, and this review does not separate the two.

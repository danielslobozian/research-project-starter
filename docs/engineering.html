01 / The question

Does a research protocol actually help?

I had built a set of instructions to make AI-assisted research more disciplined: agree the question, record explicit Research Boundaries (RB: an approved scope file), evaluate evidence, and preserve structured artifacts. The natural assumption was that using the full protocol would produce better reports.

Rather than present that assumption as a feature claim, I built a benchmark to test it. The experiment separated the contribution of defining a research brief from the contribution of executing the entire protocol.

“A useful benchmark should be able to contradict the hypothesis it was built to test.”

The first published edition is not a general-purpose leaderboard. It is a controlled evaluation of three ways to approach the same research question, with an explicit record of what was and was not measured.

CONTROLLED COMPARISON

Why three arms mattered

Arm A provided a casual-question baseline. Arm B isolated the value of the approved RB. Arm C added the full research protocol to the same RB. Five runs per arm/topic made comparisons more informative than a single anecdotal completion.

PARTITIONING THE EVIDENCE

Why topic sets mattered

Five shared topics supported cross-model comparisons. Larger 55-topic sets for selected models supported richer within-model comparisons. The edition explicitly states which conclusions use which topic set.

04 / Evaluation pipeline

One judge. An explicit evaluation process.

The edition used one LLM judge, Claude Opus 5.5, with fixed grading rules v4.1. Blinding, repeated comparisons, source spot checks, grader validation and uncertainty estimates make that judge’s preferences inspectable; they do not create independent judge agreement.

01 · GRADING CONTRACT

Structured side-by-side comparisons

Reports for the same question were presented with model labels withheld and their order randomized outside the judge. Sessions compared two to four reports and produced pairwise preference verdicts after bounded source checks.

02 · GRADER VALIDATION

Test the evaluator itself

Before the edition, the grader was checked for repeat consistency and its ability to recognize deliberately degraded reports. Its v4.1 rules were then fixed for the panel.

03 · STATISTICS

From verdicts to uncertainty

The published analysis fits Bradley–Terry model scores and topic-bootstrap confidence intervals to the comparison data. The ranking remains relative to this edition.

A limitation worth saying plainly

Claude Opus 5.5 was the sole judge and also a tested model family; five common topics underpin the cross-model ranking. Judge preference is not an objective measure of scientific truth. Grading covered the Markdown reports, without independently validating the JSON companion, diagrams or every feature of the method.

05 / Operational reliability

What went wrong—and what got published.

The work included diagnosing imperfect runs and deciding which records belonged in a valid edition. Publication included the exclusions, not just the successful jobs.

SEARCH FAILURE

Local retrieval broke

Free search providers blocked the lab's metasearch. The local rounds moved to Brave Search behind a cache; earlier rounds without working search were not published.

OUTPUT VALIDATION

Notes mistaken for reports

Seven GLM Flash jobs left only a checkpoint or notes, initially mistaken for reports by the grader. The issue was found before release; the jobs were marked failed and affected verdicts excluded.

ARCHIVE INTEGRITY

Miscollected files caught

A collection script copied four files from older job folders. These were identified before grading and removed from the published inputs.

These incidents and additional deviations remain in the edition card. The failed-job register ↗ records seven GLM jobs and eleven Gemma jobs; affected verdicts are excluded from the analyses.

07 / What I learned

Measure the assumption. Then revise the recommendation.

The published edition found an RB-only improvement over the informal question for 11 of 12 models. Adding the full engine to the RB improved judged reports in three of 13 configurations, showed no measurable difference in seven, and worsened them in three. Those 13 configurations include Sol at two effort settings; they are not 13 distinct models.

That observation informed the version 1.6 recommendation: preserve the research-defining step, then perform the investigation from the RB in a fresh session. Full-engine execution remains available for its additional structure and machine-readable companion. Report-quality grading did not independently validate that companion. Later engine development is separate from this immutable October 2026 result.

This was the first published edition of a benchmarking approach intended to be reused. The lasting result is not a favorite model or a permanent ranking; it is an experimental framework that can be refined and applied to other systems.

“The benchmark was useful because the results changed what I recommended—not because they confirmed everything I expected.”

# 1. Research objective and motivation

A daily target of 10,000 steps is widely quoted in health messaging and on consumer wearable devices. It is unclear how well that specific figure is supported by research, whether health benefits depend on reaching it, and what the evidence implies for mostly sedentary office workers. The investigation examines the origin of the figure and the strength of the evidence on daily step counts and health, without assuming that the figure is either well founded or unfounded.

# 2. Primary research question

**Q0.** How is daily step count associated with health outcomes in adults, and does the evidence support 10,000 steps a day as a necessary or especially meaningful threshold?

# 3. Secondary research questions

**Q1.** Where did the 10,000-step figure come from, and what evidence originally backed it?

**Q2.** What does the evidence show about the relationship between steps per day and all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease and other major health outcomes, including where benefits level off?

**Q3.** Does walking pace or intensity matter beyond total step count?

**Q4.** How strong is the evidence? This covers observational versus trial evidence, confounding, reverse causation and the accuracy of step-counting devices.

**Q5.** What do the findings imply for mostly sedentary adults aged roughly 25 to 60?

# 4. Included scope

- Populations: general adult populations, with age-specific findings noted; the reader context is mostly sedentary office workers aged roughly 25 to 60.
- Outcomes: all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease and other major health outcomes.
- Comparison: lower versus higher daily step counts, including sedentary baselines, and 10,000 steps versus other levels.
- Pace or intensity of walking, as addressed by Q3.
- Other activity measures (such as active minutes) only as a comparison to steps.
- Time range: the historical origin of the 10,000-step figure, plus public research available at the actual search date.

# 5. Exclusions

- People with diagnosed illnesses.
- Athletes.
- People under 25.
- Weight-loss programmes and individual medical advice.
- Findings describe groups and associations and do not prescribe a target for any individual.

# 6. Research approach and evidence expectations

- Review type: structured narrative evidence review; not an exhaustive or systematic review.
- Sources: public research and other authoritative primary material appropriate to each claim, prioritising meta-analyses, large cohort studies and randomised trials where they exist. For Q1, the historical record of the figure's origin is traced to the earliest sources that can be inspected.
- Appraisal: compare study designs and populations, and weigh each finding by design, size, measurement method and risk of confounding and reverse causation.
- Uncertainty: state where evidence is mixed, thin or limited to certain groups, and distinguish association from causation.
- Access limits: only sources that can actually be inspected are cited; unreachable questions are reported as such.

# 9. Assumptions, uncertainties, and open boundaries

- The reading copy is a PDF, as requested; the Markdown source accompanies it.
- The 6-page limit is an estimate converted from a reading time of about 15 minutes (roughly 3,000 words at 200 to 230 words per minute); the 25% tolerance allows up to 7.5 pages.
- Other activity measures appear only as comparison to steps.
- The 10,000-step figure is treated as a premise to test, not as an established fact or an established myth.
- Findings on populations outside the 25-to-60 range are used only where they bear on the accepted questions, with the age difference noted.
- Web access and PDF production were available at the capability check and are rechecked at execution; any gap found there is reported in the delivery.

# 1. Research objective and motivation

Assess the scientific evidence regarding screen exposure and children's brain development, cognition, behaviour, and well-being, and translate appropriately supported conclusions into practical family rules for tablet and television use. Examine both potential benefits and potential harms. Distinguish demonstrated developmental effects from associations, plausible but unproven mechanisms, and public claims that may overstate or understate the available evidence; do not presume either that screens damage developing brains or that concern about screen use is merely a moral panic.

# 2. Primary research question

**Q0.** What does scientific evidence establish about the effects of screen exposure on children's brain development, cognition, and well-being, and what conclusions are justified for setting screen-use rules for a six-year-old child?

# 3. Secondary research questions

**Q1. Brain development:** Is there credible evidence that screen exposure causes harmful changes in developing brains? What can brain scans actually establish?

**Q2. Learning and behaviour:** What evidence links screen use to attention, memory, language, learning, emotional regulation, and behaviour?

**Q3. Amount:** Does the duration of daily screen use affect outcomes? Is there evidence supporting particular limits or thresholds?

**Q4. Content and activity:** How do educational programmes, entertainment, games, interactive applications, and passive television differ in their effects?

**Q5. Context and timing:** How do bedtime viewing, background television, parental involvement, and activities displaced by screens influence outcomes?

**Q6. Evidence versus alarm:** Which prominent concerns are well supported, uncertain, contradicted, or amplified beyond the available evidence?

**Q7. Practical guidance:** What screen-use rules concerning duration, content, and timing are justified for a six-year-old, and how do French recommendations compare with the research?

# 4. Included scope

- Children approximately ages 5–7, with particular attention to decisions for a six-year-old living in France. Evidence from adjacent age groups may be included where directly informative, with applicability to this age made explicit.
- Tablet and television use in everyday family settings, including time spent, type of content and activity, timing, family context, and potential displacement of other activities.
- Potential benefits as well as harms, including brain development and cognition, learning, attention, language, emotional regulation, behaviour, and well-being to the extent addressed by the approved questions.
- Relevant research from France and internationally; French public-health recommendations and the evidential basis for those recommendations.
- Comparisons necessary to determine whether findings support particular amounts, types, or timing of screen use, and whether claims exceed what the evidence can establish.

# 5. Exclusions

- Adolescent social-media risks, except where directly relevant to interpreting evidence for the target age.
- Unrelated issues involving adult screen use.
- Reviews or rankings of individual commercial applications.
- Individual medical diagnosis or treatment.
- Generalization from different ages, exposures, or settings to six-year-olds without discussing the limits of applicability.

# 6. Research approach and evidence expectations

Conduct a structured scientific evidence review, not an exhaustive systematic review. Use relevant scientific studies and authoritative primary material, including French public-health guidance; cover foundational research and pertinent developments available at the actual search date. Evaluate study design, outcome measures, quality, replication or consistency, competing explanations, and uncertainty. Make the causal strength of each major conclusion explicit.

In particular:

- Distinguish observational associations from causal evidence, considering confounding factors and the direction of possible effects.
- Distinguish experimental interventions from observational and longitudinal studies, explaining what each design can and cannot establish.
- Distinguish neuroimaging or other brain measurements from demonstrated impairment or clinically meaningful harm; avoid interpreting a measured difference as damage without supporting evidence.
- Compare evidence concerning duration, thresholds, content, interactive versus passive use, bedtime exposure, background television, parental involvement, and displaced activities where relevant to the approved questions.
- Separate scientific findings from precautionary or value-based public-health recommendations; examine how French recommendations relate to underlying evidence.
- Assess contrary and null findings as well as reported harms or benefits. Explain uncertainty and avoid presenting an exact daily limit as scientifically established unless supported by evidence.

Conclude with an evidence-weighted guide for family decisions about tablet and TV duration, content, and timing, identifying which suggestions are strongly supported, precautionary, or uncertain. This is decision support, not diagnosis or a claim that one rule is universally appropriate.

# 7. Status of examples

No scope-defining examples supplied. Tablet and television use are included exposures, not illustrative cases to be substituted with unrelated digital media. Bedtime viewing, background television, parental involvement, and educational versus entertainment content are comparisons explicitly named in the approved brief.

# 9. Assumptions, uncertainties, and open boundaries

- The evidence review focuses on roughly ages 5–7, rather than attempting equal coverage of every childhood age; studies of other ages are used only where directly informative.
- The report addresses generally applicable family decision-making for a six-year-old in France, not a personalized clinical assessment; individual developmental or medical circumstances have not been specified.
- There is no fixed source corpus or date restriction beyond including foundational and pertinent research available at the actual search date.
- Whether any particular exposure, effect, or numerical screen-time threshold is causally supported is an open research question, not an assumed finding.
- No further scope or presentation questions remained open at approval of BR-001.

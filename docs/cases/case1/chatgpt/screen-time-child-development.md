# Screen time and development at age six: what the evidence supports

*Scientific evidence, uncertainty, and practical tablet and television rules for families in France*  
*English · 9 November 2026*

## The answer in brief

A television or tablet is **not proven to damage a child's brain**, and a brain-scan difference is not proof of injury. Yet screen experiences are not interchangeable: studies find generally **small associations** between heavier or poorly contextualized use and some language, learning, behavioural, and sleep outcomes. Some experiments strengthen the case for managing **what is watched** and for protecting time for family interaction, play, and sleep. Other well-designed studies find weak or **no clear associations** for certain outcomes. These findings rule out both “all screens are harmless” and “every minute is neurotoxic.” [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3)

For children around six, the best-supported approach is to **plan limited leisure use, choose age-appropriate material, participate or discuss it, avoid background television and screen-centred meals, and keep screens out of bedtime routines**. There is **no established clinical cutoff** proving that, for example, 29 minutes is safe while 61 minutes is harmful. A household limit is a practical way to preserve other activities, not a scientifically identified toxicity threshold. [[2]](#ref-2) [[15]](#ref-15) [[18]](#ref-18)

French national guidance places **ages 6–9** in a “limited and supervised” category, with no personal screen and no solo internet access; ages **3–6** are treated more restrictively, with use discouraged and exceptional. Around the sixth birthday, that transition calls for judgment rather than a sudden change in biology. French rules prohibiting screens in early-childhood care settings concern **children under three**, not a legal ban on a six-year-old using a family television. [[20]](#ref-20) [[22]](#ref-22)

**Practical starting point:** choose a scheduled, supervised programme or activity on some days (for example, a **20–40-minute slot** after school), with screen-free meals, bedrooms, and the hour before sleep. This duration is an **illustrative family rule, not an evidence-proven safe dose**; some families may reasonably choose less or none on weekdays. Review whether sleep, reading, active play, school routines, and family time are protected before adjusting the rule. [[18]](#ref-18) [[19]](#ref-19) [[20]](#ref-20)

## 1. Scope, method, and how to interpret a study

This structured, non-exhaustive scientific review asks **Q0: What does scientific evidence establish about the effects of screen exposure on children's brain development, cognition, and well-being, and what conclusions are justified for setting screen-use rules for a six-year-old child?** It addresses **Q1–Q7** below. Its focus is everyday **tablet and television** use at roughly ages **5–7**, with relevant younger or older research explicitly identified. It does not evaluate individual apps, diagnose a child, or generalize evidence on adolescent social media to early primary school. “Screen time” may mean passive TV, a carefully designed educational activity, or a distracting stream of short videos: a single number hides these differences.

**Review process.** Sources were located and examined through targeted public-web searches on **9 November 2026** using combinations of *screen use/time, preschool, school entry, development, language, cognition, executive function, imaging, randomized trial, systematic review, bedtime, background television, parent co-use*, and *French national screen guidance*. The selection prioritized original studies with relevant age groups, controlled experiments, longitudinal cohorts, systematic reviews, recent umbrella reviews, and French official material. Reference trails within the reviews were used to locate major original trials. The decisive findings were checked in available full-text journal HTML or accessible article abstracts; some publisher/PMC full texts could not be opened. A particularly relevant new **2026** child-language cohort and 2026 syntheses were included. This is **not** a database-complete systematic review, and no independent peer review or new experiment was performed.

Four research terms are worth knowing. A **cross-sectional** study measures exposure and outcome around the same time and cannot establish which came first. A **cohort** follows children over time and can test whether one predicts the other, but changes in family circumstances and unmeasured factors can still confuse the result. A **randomized controlled trial (RCT)** assigns families to different approaches by chance; differences afterward are stronger evidence of a causal *intervention effect*, although the intervention may change several things simultaneously. A **meta-analysis** pools results of other studies; it makes evidence easier to see but cannot remove biases that those studies share. An **umbrella review** summarizes previous reviews, which may contain overlapping children and studies; its headline sample size is not a new experiment. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[5]](#ref-5) [[8]](#ref-8)

Studies frequently report a **correlation, r**, from −1 to +1. A value near zero means a weak linear relationship across a sample, not “no possible effect for anyone”; **r = −0.14** means a small negative association, not a **14% loss** of language ability. A **95% confidence interval (CI)** expresses the statistical uncertainty under the analysis; an interval spanning zero does not establish that there is no effect. A **standardized difference** (Cohen's *d*) describes separation in units of the outcome's standard deviation, not minutes or a probability of harm. Statistical significance is not the same as importance for an individual child. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[9]](#ref-9)

**Evidence-grading language here:** *experimental support for a specific short-term change* is stronger than *consistent adjusted association*; *suggestive* indicates limited transfer, heterogeneous measures, or uncertain causality; *not demonstrated* means the investigated evidence does not justify the claim, not that the opposite is proven. Conclusions are grouped by practical consequence rather than by how alarming the finding sounds.

## 2. Q1 — Does screen exposure harm the developing brain? What scans establish

**Answer: Harmful brain changes caused by ordinary screen exposure have not been demonstrated.** Scanning studies can detect group differences in tissue-sensitive or connectivity measures. They cannot by themselves show that viewing caused them, that a child's brain was injured, or that an observed difference will persist. The important distinction is **“associated brain measurement” versus “demonstrated clinical impairment.”** [[6]](#ref-6) [[18]](#ref-18)

A widely reported US study scanned **47 children aged three to five** and compared MRI-derived **white-matter diffusion** measurements with parents' answers to a 15-item screen-use questionnaire. White matter consists of pathways linking brain regions; diffusion MRI estimates how water moves in tissue and provides *indirect* indicators of its organization. Higher questionnaire scores correlated with some diffusion measures and with lower language/early-literacy scores, after specified adjustments. The questionnaire bundled access, content, frequency, and co-viewing; it was **not** a randomized test of a precise daily viewing dose. Age and household income were adjusted for in the MRI analysis, but the small cross-sectional sample could not remove all alternative explanations. Even the authors called for multiyear research. These results justify investigation, **not the headline “screens damage white matter.”** [[6]](#ref-6)

A plausible mechanism is **displacement**: an hour absorbed in passive entertainment may be an hour not used to talk, read, build, imagine, move, or practise social skills. Another is altered interaction when adults or background TV repeatedly interrupt play. These pathways are plausible and some immediate interaction effects are experimental, but a mechanism is not itself proof of lasting brain injury. Brain development also reflects sleep, schooling, health, family circumstances and genetic differences that may influence both media use and outcomes. [[5]](#ref-5) [[12]](#ref-12) [[13]](#ref-13)

**Confidence:** high that the often-repeated *brain-damage claim is stronger than the evidence*; low that current child brain imaging establishes a specific harmful causal pathway or safe exposure threshold for six-year-olds. This does **not** prove all exposures harmless.

## 3. Q2 — Attention, memory, language, learning, emotions and behaviour

**Answer: Some developmental outcomes show small average negative associations with heavier screen use; the pattern is neither uniform nor automatically causal.** A September **2026** umbrella review combined **23 meta-analyses** (underlying population totals reported as approximately **2.82 million** children across 64 countries, not independent new observations). It found correlations of **r = −0.14** with language skills and **r = −0.15** with overall academic achievement; greater screen exposure correlated with more internalizing (**r = 0.10**) and externalizing behaviour problems (**r = 0.11**). Yet its pooled executive-function correlation was **r = 0.01 (95% CI −0.09 to 0.11)**, and reading, mathematics, and science results did not establish clear associations. Ages, platforms, content and study designs varied, many data were simultaneous observations, and results are **not effect sizes for six-year-olds alone**. [[1]](#ref-1)

**Language.** A 2020 review of **42 studies / 18,905 participants** associated longer exposure with lower language scores (**r = −0.14, 95% CI −0.18 to −0.10**). Educational viewing was associated with better language (**r = 0.13, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.24**), but the studies were heterogeneous and mainly observational. The publisher later corrected accidentally reversed **axis labels** in figures, not a demonstrated reversal of those pooled estimates. This is a useful warning against treating a graphic as a biological law. [[9]](#ref-9) [[10]](#ref-10)

**Development over time.** In the Canadian *All Our Families* cohort, **2,441** children were assessed around ages **2, 3 and 5**. A repeated-measures model found small associations from higher screen exposure at an earlier age to lower later developmental-screening scores (**standardized β = −0.06** and **−0.08** for the successive time intervals), while the opposite paths were not detected. Tracking the same children improves on a single snapshot, but both screen hours and screening outcomes came from parent reports, and no child was randomized to watch more TV. The original article was corrected in **2019** for numerical errors; the figures and estimates quoted here follow its updated online version. [[5]](#ref-5)

**Evidence particularly close to six.** The French **ELFE** birth cohort studied **13,763** children with at least one measure, using parental media reports at ages **2, 3½ and 5½** and several cognitive tests or questionnaires. After adjustments, associations of total use with cognitive scores were generally **weak**; TV during family meals at age two predicted some poorer language/cognitive outcomes, while **screen exposure at 3½ did not predict the overall developmental-inventory score at 5½** in the adjusted longitudinal comparison. A different, concurrent nonverbal reasoning result was *positive*. This does not mean TV at meals is proven causal or that screens improve reasoning; it means the evidence is more mixed than a uniform-harm narrative. [[3]](#ref-3)

A **2026** Norwegian cohort followed **747 children** from about age **5 to 7**. **Most** child- and parent-screen indicators did **not** predict growth in receptive vocabulary or grammar. The primary parent's smartphone exposure was associated with **0.42 fewer raw vocabulary-score points of growth per year per additional reported hour/day** (95% CI −0.66 to −0.18), and owning a device was linked to lower *baseline* language scores. These are **associations**: family routines or pre-existing child differences could affect both ownership and performance, and parent phone use was not experimentally assigned. This near-age study is an important counterexample to a blanket prediction that a six-year-old's daily screen duration must lower language growth. [[4]](#ref-4)

**Attention, memory and self-control.** An RCT with **60 four-year-olds** found worse immediate executive-function task performance after **nine minutes** of one fast-paced cartoon than after an educational cartoon or drawing. It cannot establish months-long attention deficits or effects for all cartoons. A later 2024 evidence review identified **mixed immediate experimental findings** across studies of fast-paced content (10 negative, 2 positive, 25 null reported findings across six experiments); its pooled observational estimate for **games/apps and overall cognition** was close to zero (**r = −0.01, 95% CI −0.12 to 0.11**). Memory-specific long-term causal effects at age six remain inadequately established. [[24]](#ref-24) [[2]](#ref-2)

**Behaviour and emotional regulation: unusually informative experiments.** In one randomized study, **565 US preschoolers aged 3–5** were assigned to a programme helping parents **replace violent or age-inappropriate television with educational and prosocial programming**, *without deliberately reducing viewing time*, or to a control intervention. At **six months**, the intervention group improved **2.11 points** more on a social-competence/behaviour scale (95% CI **0.78–3.44**); the externalizing and social-competence subscales also improved. This supports the causal benefit of the **whole content-substitution programme**, not the claim that all educational TV improves every child. The comparison included parental coaching, and the original population was younger than six. [[7]](#ref-7)

A Danish family RCT assigned **89 families / 181 children and adolescents (ages 4–17)** either to a highly supported **two-week restriction** of leisure screen media (**at most three hours per week per person**, with tablets/phones handed in) or to usual activity. Total parent-reported behavioural difficulties improved **1.67 SDQ scale points more** in the intervention group (95% CI **−2.68 to −0.67**, reported standardized **d = 0.53**); benefits were concentrated in emotional/peer difficulties and prosocial behaviour. The trial shows that **a substantial family-wide short-term change can alter measured wellbeing**, but not that three hours a week is a scientifically proven lifelong optimum. The sample was mainly older children, the trial was only two weeks long, families had opted into participation, and improved family interactions may have contributed. [[8]](#ref-8)

**Confidence:** moderate for associations in language and some psychosocial domains, with **small** average magnitudes and substantial confounding; moderately strong for *specific randomized intervention effects*, restricted to their participants and follow-up. No basis here for diagnosing an attention disorder or a permanent change from household TV exposure.

## 4. Q3 — How many minutes? Dose, thresholds and the right comparison

**Answer: More time can matter, but the evidence does not identify a precise safe/harmful daily boundary at six.** Much evidence measures self- or parent-reported **average hours**, compares children with different family environments, or pools markedly different activities. A dose association (if present) is **not** a discovered cliff at 30, 60, or 120 minutes. In particular, one hour of co-viewed literacy content and one hour of autoplay entertainment have different content, social context, and opportunity costs. [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3) [[18]](#ref-18)

Two numbers illustrate the difference between a **description** and a **prescription**. A national French ELFE analysis reported an adjusted, weighted mean of about **1 hour 34 minutes of daily screen exposure at age 5½** (95% CI **1h32–1h36**). This describes **reported behaviour in that cohort**, not an ideal amount or a health threshold. Meanwhile, the Danish randomized trial restricted families to **≤3 hours per week** for two weeks; that was an **experimental contrast** chosen to produce a measurable change, not a validated universal family rule. [[23]](#ref-23) [[8]](#ref-8)

When choosing an amount, a more informative question than “Is 45 minutes safe?” is: **What does that 45 minutes replace?** The same viewing slot might replace background TV and aimless scrolling; or it might displace the only time available for outdoor play, sleep, books, or unhurried conversation. Counting duration is still useful for predictability, limiting spirals of autoplay, and noticing changes in routines. But duration alone cannot rank the developmental value of those alternatives. [[18]](#ref-18) [[19]](#ref-19)

The **2026 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP)** policy says limits for school-aged children should fit the family and safeguard sleep, play, exercise and reading, rather than relying on a single age-wide number; its discussion allows that limits might range from under an hour for preschoolers to roughly one–two hours or more of leisure media for older children depending on context. This is **professional guidance**, not an experimentally verified threshold or the French rule. The more restrictive French recommendation for 6–9-year-olds remains “limited and supervised,” without specifying a universal minute ceiling on its age-banded page. [[18]](#ref-18) [[19]](#ref-19) [[20]](#ref-20)

**Practical interpretation:** a family can set a clear *planning cap*—for instance, one **20–40-minute** selected slot on a school day, or none when other activities take priority—then review whether it works. That number is **an openly chosen starting convention**. Neither this report nor the cited studies can promise that it prevents a particular developmental outcome. Repeated conflict when the device stops, loss of sleep, or displacement of ordinary activities may be more informative reasons to revise a plan than a small difference in daily minutes.

## 5. Q4 — Educational TV, entertainment, games, apps: different effects

**Answer: Screens are tools and media, not one exposure.** The crucial distinctions are whether a programme is understandable at the child's age, whether the child must meaningfully think or respond, whether the adult helps connect it to life, and whether the activity is intentionally chosen rather than endlessly supplied by an algorithm. A cartoon made *for children* is not automatically educational: in the 2024 context meta-analysis, **child-directed content** as a broad class had an essentially null association with cognition (**r = 0.01, 95% CI −0.05 to 0.07**). **Age-inappropriate** material correlated with poorer psychosocial results (**r = −0.11**), whereas background TV and undifferentiated programme viewing tended toward adverse correlations. [[2]](#ref-2)

**Educational programming can help with *specific* learning.** The language review found a small favourable educational-programme association, and the preschool randomized content-substitution trial changed social behaviour without a duration-reduction instruction. Neither establishes that any video marketed as “educational” is valuable. Watch for coherent stories, clear questions or explanations, age-appropriate pace, and opportunities to relate the material to books, conversation, art, or real-world discovery. [[7]](#ref-7) [[9]](#ref-9)

**Digital educational games may teach target skills, not general intelligence.** A **2026** synthesis of **36 experimental or quasi-experimental studies** from preschool to grade three reported an average **+0.31 standard deviations** on literacy/mathematics outcomes with educational apps; gains were larger on researcher-built tests of narrow skills than on broad standardized achievement tests. In a separate **2026** randomized study of **459 families with 3–5-year-olds**, giving families math apps or analogous physical math resources did **not** produce a significant **average** maths gain, although a pre-specified subgroup without college-educated parents showed a *suggestive*, statistically uncertain app benefit (~**0.17 standard deviations**, p≈0.10). Together they show **possibility and variation**, not a guarantee; these learning trials also cannot justify substituting tablets for ordinary play at home. [[16]](#ref-16) [[17]](#ref-17)

**Interactive is not synonymous with educational.** Tapping, points, ads, rapid rewards and constant prompts can distract from a lesson. In an experiment in **116 toddlers (24–36 months)**, carefully designed touch responses emphasizing the *relevant* part of a word-learning scene helped the younger toddlers, but appeared to hinder slightly older toddlers compared with passive viewing. Transfer to a six-year-old is limited; the transferable principle is to judge **what interaction teaches**, not how many screen taps occur. [[26]](#ref-26)

**Adult co-use is an advantage, not a cure-all.** The 2024 early-childhood meta-analysis associated shared use with cognition (**r = 0.14, 95% CI 0.03–0.25**). A separate 2024 review comparing adult-child co-use with non-co-use in early learning found a **small** average benefit (**Hedges' g = 0.198**, p = .009), while noting small studies and limited variety of designs. Co-watching can make a programme a prompt for explanations and questions, but sitting silently next to a child is not the same as thoughtful interaction; nor does co-use make unlimited viewing harmless. [[2]](#ref-2) [[11]](#ref-11)

## 6. Q5 — When and with whom? Bedtime, background TV, parent attention and displacement

**Answer: Protecting sleep and uninterrupted human interaction is a sounder rule than treating the total minute count as the entire problem.** This rests on a blend of direct experimental evidence for **immediate disruption** in younger children, observational associations across childhood, limited randomized sleep findings, and precautionary reasoning when extrapolating to age six.

**Background television.** In a laboratory experiment, **50 children aged 12–36 months** played while an adult programme was on during one part of a session and off during another. With TV on, their toy-play episodes and focused attention were shorter, though they scarcely looked at the TV. Another experiment with **51 similarly young children** found fewer and lower-quality parent-child interactions while background television was on. These controlled within-session contrasts support immediate distraction in *toddlers*, but do not prove lasting deficits in six-year-olds. In a 2024 observational meta-analysis of studies up to age six, background TV correlated weakly with poorer cognition (**r = −0.10, 95% CI −0.18 to −0.02**). Turning it off when nobody is intentionally watching is a low-cost, well-grounded measure. [[12]](#ref-12) [[13]](#ref-13) [[2]](#ref-2)

**Mealtimes and adult attention.** The French ELFE cohort found that a TV on during meals at age two, rather than overall screen duration at that age, predicted some lower early language/development scores after adjustment. The 2024 context meta-analysis associated caregiver use during routines with poorer psychosocial outcomes (**r = −0.11, 95% CI −0.20 to −0.03**). A newer **five-to-seven-year-old** Norwegian study similarly raised concern about heavy parental smartphone use, while finding mostly null child-duration predictors for language growth. None proves that a particular parent's device caused a given child's outcomes, but shared meals and engaged conversation are sensible to protect. [[3]](#ref-3) [[2]](#ref-2) [[4]](#ref-4)

**Bedtime.** Across **25 daily within-person studies / 4,562 participants aged 3–25**, a **2026** synthesis found that on days with greater screen use, bedtime was slightly later on average (**r = 0.079, 95% CI 0.010–0.149**) but found **no significant pooled daily association** with total sleep time, sleep-onset latency, efficiency, awakenings or self-reported quality. Use *after* bedtime was associated with slightly poorer subjective sleep quality. This is a caution against overstating the nightly sleep effects of every pre-dinner programme, especially for a six-year-old, since much of the sample was older. [[15]](#ref-15)

An RCT of **105 UK toddlers aged 16–30 months** tested removing screens in the hour before bed and substituting activities such as reading and puzzles, compared with usual routines or provision of the same activity box without instructions to remove screens. It found **modest, preliminary** improvements in objectively measured sleep efficiency, stronger versus the **active activity-box comparison** than versus usual care; it found **no objective attention improvement**. This reinforces a reasonable **screen-free final hour** as a precaution, but cannot establish a precise one-hour biological threshold for school-aged children. [[14]](#ref-14)

**What is displaced.** In the Danish family trial, changing leisure-screen routines also altered opportunities for family interaction and other leisure pursuits. In the AAP's 2026 evidence report, the principal concern is not simply emitted light but the interaction of content, design and displaced activities. A family rule works best when it explicitly **makes room for sleep, active movement, reading, real conversation, imaginative play and self-directed boredom**, rather than replacing screens with pressure to perform. The relative contributions of light, arousal, displacement and family conflict to an individual outcome are often unmeasured. [[8]](#ref-8) [[18]](#ref-18)

## 7. Q6 — Evidence versus alarm: claim-by-claim check

**Answer: Some warnings are warranted, but major public claims often overreach the study designs.** Table 1 separates the strongest support from what remains open. “Not established” does not mean “impossible.”

**Table 1. Claims, evidence strength, and limits (judgments based on the reviewed studies, not a formal certainty-grading system).**

| Public claim | What the examined evidence supports | Decision-relevant qualification |
|:--|:--|:--|
| “Tablets or TV damage a child's brain.” | **Not demonstrated.** Small observational imaging study detected correlated brain measurements. [[6]](#ref-6) | Imaging association is not proof of injury or causation. |
| “Heavier screen use is associated with poorer language or behaviour.” | **Supported as an average association**, generally small, with variation by outcome. [[1]](#ref-1) [[9]](#ref-9) | Cannot predict individual harm or exclude family confounding. |
| “Brief, well-chosen educational screens can teach something.” | **Supported for particular learning tasks and interventions.** [[7]](#ref-7) [[16]](#ref-16) | Marketing label “educational” is insufficient; outcomes and alternatives matter. |
| “Interactive games are universally better than television.” | **Unsupported.** General app/gaming correlations include null results. [[2]](#ref-2) [[26]](#ref-26) | Meaningful task design matters more than touch input. |
| “A short family reduction may improve some behaviour.” | **Causally supported for one intensive, two-week multi-age family intervention.** [[8]](#ref-8) | Not a lifelong dose rule or proof of six-year-old-specific effect. |
| “Screens right before bed always destroy sleep.” | **Overstated.** Later bedtimes correlate slightly with screen-heavy days; short-term sleep measures often null. [[14]](#ref-14) [[15]](#ref-15) | Screen-free wind-down remains low-cost precaution. |
| “One hour daily is a scientifically proven safe limit.” | **Unsupported for age six.** [[18]](#ref-18) [[20]](#ref-20) | Numeric rules are family/public-health planning conventions. |
| “A TV playing in the background is equivalent to no TV.” | **Contradicted for immediate toddler play/interaction.** [[12]](#ref-12) [[13]](#ref-13) | Lasting effects in six-year-olds still uncertain. |

**Why the research produces conflicting headlines.** First, *exposure* differs: TV running in the room, adult device use, a selected lesson, and fast-paced autoplay are not substitutes in a study. Second, *outcomes* differ: a language screening questionnaire and a directly measured vocabulary growth score do not ask the same question. Third, *age* matters: what is true at 18 months might not be true at six or 12 years. Fourth, *association and intervention* answer different questions: replacing violent programmes can cause a measurable benefit without proving every minute of television is harmful. Finally, unusually large review samples often reuse overlapping observational studies; they do not convert correlations into experiments. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3) [[7]](#ref-7)

**Contrary and null findings deserve equal space.** In 2026, a large umbrella review found **no clear overall executive-function link**, a cohort specifically spanning ages **5–7** found **mostly null screen-duration links to language growth**, and a preschool app trial found **no average improvement** over its control. The correct response is to narrow the claim, not to dismiss either positive interventions or plausible risks. [[1]](#ref-1) [[4]](#ref-4) [[17]](#ref-17)

## 8. Q7 — An evidence-weighted family guide in France

**Answer: Set rules that protect relationships, sleep and other activities; keep screen choices intentional; treat numerical limits as adaptable tools.** France offers a particularly relevant set of **age-banded precautions**, which do not map to one experimentally established exposure threshold.

The French Ministry of Education's **2 September 2025** guidance states that screens at **ages 3–6** are **discouraged and exceptional**, with educational material and an adult. At **ages 6–9** they are **limited and supervised**, with clear rules, **no personal screen and no internet alone**. It also advises no screen **before school, during meals, in bedrooms, or before bed**, and prioritizes reading, movement, creative activity, family interactions and play. The 2025 national health booklet advises avoiding screens in the **hour before bedtime** for the younger-child group. These are **public-health guidance** and precautionary choices, not proof that an individual minute is toxic. [[20]](#ref-20) [[21]](#ref-21)

**Legal precision.** A French order dated **27 June 2025**, published **2 July**, amended the early-childhood childcare charter to **prohibit exposing under-threes to screens in that professional childcare setting**. It does **not** criminalize all screen use at home and does **not** establish a statutory screen ban for six-year-olds. This distinction matters when public discussion compresses “not recommended” and “legally prohibited.” [[22]](#ref-22)

**Table 2. Suggested house rules, with the evidential status made explicit.**

| Household choice | Specific workable rule | Evidence status and trade-off |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **How much** | Plan a known slot, e.g. **20–40 minutes on some schooldays**; no need for a daily entitlement. Weekends can differ if other activities remain protected. | **Practical/precautionary**, not an established dose threshold. A predictable endpoint can help family routines. [[18]](#ref-18) [[20]](#ref-20) |
| **What** | Choose a programme, film excerpt, story or game **before** starting; prefer age-appropriate, clearly useful or genuinely enjoyable content; avoid violent/adult content and autoplay. | **Moderate experimental support** for replacing unsuitable content; educational benefits depend on target skill. [[7]](#ref-7) [[16]](#ref-16) |
| **Together** | Watch or play together when feasible; ask what happened, predict what comes next, extend an idea into a book, drawing or real-world activity. | **Small positive associations**, plus some experimental co-use benefits; not a requirement to comment constantly. [[2]](#ref-2) [[11]](#ref-11) |
| **Meals/background** | Turn TV off if nobody is watching; keep shared meals screen-free; put adults' devices aside for attentive interaction. | **Experimental immediate disruption evidence in toddlers;** observational support in younger and near-age cohorts. [[12]](#ref-12) [[13]](#ref-13) [[3]](#ref-3) [[4]](#ref-4) |
| **Timing** | No device in the bedroom; aim for **one hour of screen-free wind-down** and no screen immediately before school. | **Prudent guidance**; sleep evidence is mixed and a one-hour cutoff is not biologically validated for age six. [[14]](#ref-14) [[15]](#ref-15) [[20]](#ref-20) |
| **Ownership/control** | Use a shared family TV or adult-controlled tablet; disable autoplay, use an age-appropriate profile, and do not allow solo internet access. | **French official 6–9 guidance** plus plausible design-risk prevention; device ownership associations are not causal. [[20]](#ref-20) [[4]](#ref-4) |

**An ordinary weekday illustration.** After a snack and an opportunity for outside or imaginative play, an adult offers a **single preselected 25-minute programme** or a focused shared tablet activity. The device goes away at the agreed ending, and dinner and the approach to bedtime stay screen-free. If the child would rather build, read, go outdoors or talk, there need not be a screen slot at all. This illustration is a *possible routine*, **not** a clinical recommendation that 25 minutes is optimal. It puts the French principles into an easy-to-remember arrangement. [[20]](#ref-20)

**What to observe rather than calculate.** Over several weeks, look for sufficient sleep and a manageable bedtime; time for physical play, reading, friendship and conversation; and whether stopping an activity remains reasonably workable. If screen sessions reliably crowd out those basics, shortening or moving them is justified even if the nominal limit looks modest. If a calm, purposeful shared programme fits without displacement, current evidence does not establish that the family has thereby harmed brain development. The goal is **manageable, attentive family life**, not fear of a timer. [[18]](#ref-18) [[19]](#ref-19)

**Three levels of recommendation:** (1) **Better supported:** select age-appropriate/prosocial content rather than violent or unsuitable programmes, reduce distracting background TV, and preserve interaction. (2) **Reasonable precaution:** screen-free meals/bedrooms/last hour, shared-device control, and planned time slots. (3) **Not scientifically settled:** exact 20-, 30-, 60-, or 90-minute maximums and irreversible neurological harm from ordinary use. The French guidance can be followed without pretending all three levels have identical empirical status.

## 9. Research limits and what would change the conclusion

The strongest limits are **age transfer**, **measurement** and **causality**. Much foundational language and background-TV research involves **infants/toddlers**, not children at school entry. Several large meta-analyses combine ages up to 12, 18 or 25 and mixed media, so their correlations cannot be directly read as six-year-old effect sizes. Parent-reported exposure may omit background TV, shared screens or specific content; questionnaire outcomes may share reporting bias with exposure. Families who use screens more may differ in stress, schedules, resources, sleep, childcare, or existing child behaviour even after statistical adjustment. Some studies offer many related tests; small statistically significant differences may not be clinically meaningful. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3) [[5]](#ref-5) [[15]](#ref-15)

Review-level evidence is useful but not a substitute for primary-study appraisal. For example, a 2026 umbrella review of **121 reviews / more than nine million underlying participants** judged about **71%** of reviews low or critically low in methodological quality and reported low/very-low **GRADE** certainty for about **92%**. Counts at that scale frequently overlap, and much material concerns ages older than this report's target. The **2024** context review itself rated **65 of its 100 studies weak**, and found substantial variation between study results for several comparisons. These limits temper the meaning of precise-looking pooled decimals. [[25]](#ref-25) [[2]](#ref-2)

**Access and selection limitations.** Full journal HTML was inspected for several core original studies and reviews; for some recent or paywalled records, only a publisher-hosted abstract or full indexed excerpt could be assessed. This review did not systematically screen every database record, reproduce pooled analyses, or independently reanalyse source data. It verified the published **2022 correction to figure labels** for the 2020 language review and used the corrected published results for the 2019 longitudinal cohort, but has not audited every source for every subsequent notice. The references identify material inspected and relevant limitations. An individual study's absence from this selected body is not a finding that it does not exist.

A stronger answer for six-year-olds would require replicated **preregistered** trials that separately vary **duration and content** while preserving equivalent parent attention and alternative activities; longer follow-up; objective exposure records; clearly specified language, attention and sleep outcomes; and transparent analyses by child age and prior behaviour. Longer-term experimental evidence might refine the advice about *how much*. Unless such evidence shows a consistent harm threshold, a single clinical daily cutoff would remain unsubstantiated.

<a id="q0"></a>
## 10. Q0 — Final synthesis and decision

For a six-year-old, the totality of inspected research supports **intentional, limited, supervised, age-appropriate use**, not claims of proven brain damage or a precise safe-dose cutoff. Screen duration is a useful **household management variable**, but content, shared attention, background exposure, timing and displaced activities are central to understanding likely benefits and costs. Small observational links coexist with null findings and useful randomized evidence that changing media content or family routines can make a difference. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[4]](#ref-4) [[7]](#ref-7) [[8]](#ref-8)

The most defensible rule is therefore **“protect the important things first, choose the screen activity second.”** France's 6–9 framework gives a practical foundation: no personal device, no unsupervised internet, clear limits, and engaged adult oversight; keep meals, bedrooms and bedtime free of screens. Within that framework, a short scheduled TV episode or shared tablet activity can be a choice rather than a developmental emergency, provided it does not take over sleep, play or relationships. The hour count remains **adaptable and precautionary**, not a claim of scientific certainty. [[18]](#ref-18) [[20]](#ref-20)

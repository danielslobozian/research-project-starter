# 1. Research objective and motivation

Public discussion presents children's screen use both as a threat to the developing brain and as the latest moral panic about a new medium. The investigation establishes what the evidence supports for tablet and TV use in young children, and how well public concern matches that evidence, so that the results can inform decisions about household rules.

The following premises are tested rather than assumed:

- "Harmful" and "moral panic" may not be either/or, since both could be partly true.
- "Screen time" may not be one thing. Amount, content and context may matter separately.
- Differences seen in brain scans may or may not mean harm.
- Children who watch more may differ in other ways, so links may not show cause.

# 2. Primary research question

**Q0.** How strong is the evidence that tablet and TV use harms, benefits, or makes no meaningful difference to young children's brain development and thinking skills, and how well does public concern match that evidence?

# 3. Secondary research questions

**Q1.** What can brain-scan studies show, and do they show cause?

**Q2.** What are the effects on attention, language, self-control, learning and behaviour, and how large are they?

**Q3.** Do effects depend on how much, what kind of content, when (including before bed and effects on sleep), and whether an adult joins in?

**Q4.** How much of any effect comes from what screens replace, such as sleep, play or reading?

**Q5.** Does the debate resemble past media panics, and where do claims go beyond the evidence?

**Q6.** What do French and international health bodies recommend, and on what evidence?

**Q7.** Which options for family rules does the evidence support, and how confidently?

# 4. Included scope

- Population: children of about 3–12, weighted toward ages 5–8.
- Media: tablets, TV and video, including games and educational apps.
- Setting: use at home.
- Outcomes: brain development and thinking skills, including attention, language, self-control, learning and behaviour, and sleep.
- Guidance: recommendations of French and international health bodies.
- Debate: comparison of the current debate with past media panics.

No publication-date range was specified.

# 5. Exclusions

- Teenagers and social media, except as background.
- Diagnosis or treatment of conditions such as ADHD or autism.
- Classroom technology.
- Eyesight and weight.

# 6. Research approach and evidence expectations

The investigation is a structured narrative review, not an exhaustive one. It prioritises combined analyses of many studies, studies following the same children over years, and experiments. It separates association from cause, gives effect sizes in plain words, and says where evidence for age 6 is thin. "Evidence insufficient" is an allowed answer to any question. Paywalled sources are recorded as limitations.

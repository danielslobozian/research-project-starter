# 1. Research objective and motivation

Public and scientific debate asks whether children's screen media use harms their developing brains, and whether the surrounding alarm is justified or is a "moral panic": public fear of a new medium that runs far ahead of the evidence, as with past panics over novels, comics, radio, and television. The investigation weighs the evidence itself rather than the volume of the debate. It is motivated by a practical decision: a parent deciding family rules for a child's tablet and television use (how much, what content, when).

Premises to test rather than assume: that screen media use causes measurable harm to children's brain development or wellbeing; that any harm is uniform across the amount, content, and context of use; and that the concern is merely a moral panic with no evidential basis.

# 2. Primary research question

**Q0.** What does the current scientific evidence show about whether and how screen media use affects children's brain development, cognition, and wellbeing, and how strong is that evidence?

# 3. Secondary research questions

**Q1.** How large, consistent, and causal are the observed effects of screen media use on children, including brain structure and function, cognition (language, attention), sleep, and mood, and how do study design, confounding, and reverse causation affect what the evidence can support?

**Q2.** Does the content and context of use (what is watched or played, when, and with whom) matter more than the total amount of screen time?

**Q3.** Does the current debate fit the "moral panic" pattern, and how do researchers characterize and judge that framing?

**Q4.** What do the answers imply for practical family rules on a child's tablet and television use (how much, what content, when) for a child of about six?

# 4. Included scope

- Population: children and adolescents aged 0-18, with evidence most applicable to ages about 4-12 given the greatest weight for the practical decision.
- Screen media and uses: television, tablets, video streaming, and video games; the adolescent social-media debate is included as part of the wider evidence picture.
- Outcomes: brain development (including neuroimaging findings where available), cognition (language, attention, executive function), sleep, and mental health and wellbeing.
- Guidance: French official guidance and recommendations relevant to family screen rules, where applicable to the practical decision.
- Coverage: foundational work plus relevant developments available at the actual search date.

# 5. Exclusions

- No clinical treatment or intervention guidance for diagnosed conditions.
- No school, platform, or public-policy reform recommendations; the decision context is family rules.
- No adult screen use.
- No specific product, app, or platform marketing claims.
- Generalization is bounded by the fact that most evidence is observational (correlational), which the report must state where relevant.

# 6. Research approach and evidence expectations

Structured narrative evidence review, not an exhaustive or systematic review. Prioritize systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and large child-cohort studies; appraise study quality, causality, and effect sizes in plain terms, including the limits of cross-sectional and correlational designs and the influence of confounding and reverse causation. Weigh competing interpretations on the evidence rather than on their prominence. Public sources are used; any paywalled item is recorded as an access limitation, not bypassed or fabricated. Uncertainty is stated plainly. Every question is closed as complete, or pending/blocked with the reason.

# 7. Status of examples

No scope-defining examples supplied. The tablet and television uses named in the reader context fall within the included scope as ordinary cases, not as mandatory comparisons.

# 9. Assumptions, uncertainties, and open boundaries

- Reading copy HTML (`screen-time-child-brains.html`) was proposed in BR-001 and adopted by its approval without an explicit format preference.
- Scope spans all under-18s rather than only under-12s, adopted by approval of BR-001, because the moral-panic framing spans all ages; evidence for ages about 4-12 carries the greatest weight for the decision.
- English report for a reader living in France, adopted by approval of BR-001; French official guidance is covered with translations labelled.
- The "moral panic" framing is treated as a competing interpretation to be tested against the evidence, not as an established conclusion or a dismissed one.

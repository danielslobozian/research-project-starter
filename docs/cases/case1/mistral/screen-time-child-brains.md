# Screen Media and Children's Developing Brains: What the Evidence Shows, and How Strong It Is

*An evidence review to support family screen rules for a child of about six*

Language: English · Finalized: November 9, 2026

## Summary

Screen media use in children is linked, in large bodies of research, to slightly poorer developmental outcomes: somewhat weaker language and academic results, slightly more behaviour and mood difficulties, and clearly worse sleep [[1]](#ref-1)[[13]](#ref-13). The measured effects are small. Across 23 pooled analyses covering about 2.8 million children, typical associations are around r = 0.10 to 0.15, meaning screen use explains about 1% of the differences between children [[1]](#ref-1)[[20]](#ref-20). The evidence is strongest and most consistent for sleep: children who use or even just keep a screen device near the bed have roughly twice the odds of inadequate sleep [[3]](#ref-3), and each extra hour of daily screen time delays bedtime by about 13 minutes [[12]](#ref-12).

Most of this research is observational: it shows that heavier screen use goes together with somewhat poorer outcomes, not that one causes the other. Family circumstances, and children's own difficulties, can drive both screen use and outcomes. One high-quality study that tested both directions found screen use at ages 2 and 3 predicted slightly poorer development at 3 and 5, while the reverse was not observed [[5]](#ref-5), but this does not settle the question for all ages and outcomes.

What children watch, and whether a parent shares and talks about it, is at least as important as the clock: educational content and shared viewing are linked to *better* language, while background television, fast-paced or age-inappropriate content, and screens at bedtime are the clearest negative signals [[6]](#ref-6)[[8]](#ref-8)[[3]](#ref-3). The public debate partly fits the historical pattern of media "moral panics," but the scientific picture is neither pure panic nor cause for alarm: small average effects, concentrated in specific uses, with the largest and most reliable one being lost sleep [[14]](#ref-14)[[18]](#ref-18). For a child of about six in France, official guidance and the evidence reviewed here point to the same practical rules: no screens near bedtime or in the bedroom, age-appropriate educational content watched together, and moderate total amounts kept below play, school, sleep and family time [[4]](#ref-4)[[22]](#ref-22).

## Scope and method {#scope}

This report answers one main question and four secondary questions, defined in advance in the approved research plan (Research Boundaries file `screen-time-child-brains.rb.md`):

- **Q0.** What does the current scientific evidence show about whether and how screen media use affects children's brain development, cognition, and wellbeing, and how strong is that evidence?
- **Q1.** How large, consistent, and causal are the observed effects, including on brain structure and function, cognition, sleep, and mood?
- **Q2.** Does the content and context of use matter more than the total amount of screen time?
- **Q3.** Does the current debate fit the "moral panic" pattern?
- **Q4.** What do the answers imply for family rules on a child's tablet and television use?

The review covers children and adolescents up to 18, with the greatest weight on ages about 4 to 12, because the practical purpose is deciding family rules for a child of about six. Television, tablets, video streaming, and video games are in scope; the adolescent social-media debate is included as part of the wider evidence picture. Clinical treatment guidance, school and platform policy reform, adult screen use, and marketing claims are out of scope.

The method is a structured narrative evidence review, not an exhaustive systematic review. Priority was given to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, large child-cohort studies, and brain-imaging studies, plus the official French guidance relevant to family rules. Studies were appraised for design, confounding, and reverse causation, and competing interpretations were weighed on their evidence rather than their prominence. Public sources were used; some journal articles were paywalled, and where their full text could not be inspected the report relies on their abstracts or on clearly labelled secondary accounts, and notes this. Searching stopped when new queries on each question kept returning the same studies and the same conclusions; the stopping point is recorded here rather than in a claimed exhaustive search. Searches were performed on November 9, 2026.

## How to judge the evidence {#concepts}

Four ideas explain most of the disagreement in this field, so they come first.

**Correlation is not causation.** Much of the research observes children as they are: families who report more screen time also report somewhat poorer outcomes. This is a *correlation* (things that move together). A correlation can mean screens cause the outcome, but it can also mean the outcome causes the screen use (a child who struggles to regulate emotions may be given screens more often), or that a third factor drives both (busy or stretched households may have both more screen time and less reading time). A third factor driving both is called *confounding*.

**Study designs differ in strength.** A *cross-sectional study* measures everything at once, and cannot separate cause from effect. A *longitudinal cohort study* follows the same children over years; a "cohort" is simply a group of people followed through time. Longitudinal designs can show that screen use came *before* an outcome, which is a necessary step toward a causal claim. The strongest evidence, *randomized experiments*, where families are assigned by chance to change screen use, is scarce for broad developmental outcomes; small trials mainly show that parents can reduce preschoolers' screen time, not what the reduction achieves [[1]](#ref-1).

**A meta-analysis pools studies; an umbrella review pools meta-analyses.** A *meta-analysis* combines the numbers of many studies into one statistical estimate, usually a correlation called *r*, which ranges from −1 to +1. In this field, values around 0.10 to 0.20 are "small": real, but explaining about 1% to 4% of differences between children. The 2026 umbrella review in JAMA Pediatrics pooled 23 meta-analyses on about 2.8 million children from 64 countries, the broadest synthesis to date [[1]](#ref-1)[[20]](#ref-20). Pooling strengthens confidence that an association exists, not that it is causal: most pooled studies are still observational.

**Uncertainty is normal and should be stated.** Every pooled estimate comes with a *confidence interval* (a range within which the true value probably lies) and with *heterogeneity* (how much studies disagree). In this field heterogeneity is typically large, which means the average hides studies finding stronger, weaker, or no effects. Where estimates below have wide intervals or come from few studies, the text says so.

Two measurement cautions run through everything. "Screen time" is usually reported by parents, and lumps together a cartoon, a video call with grandparents, and homework videos into one number; researchers have criticized this "screen time" category as conceptually weak for years [[19]](#ref-19). And screens displace other activities: sleep, play, conversation. A finding such as "each screen hour delays bedtime by 13 minutes" [[12]](#ref-12) is partly a statement about time, not about screens as a substance.

## Q0: What the evidence shows overall {#q0}

**Answer: screen media use is associated with somewhat poorer development and wellbeing on average, the effects are small, the sleep evidence is the most robust, and the causal evidence is limited.** {#q0-answer}

The umbrella review of 2.8 million children found small negative associations between screen use and academic achievement (r = −0.15), language (r = −0.14), and internalizing problems such as anxiety and low mood (r = 0.10), and small positive associations with externalizing problems such as aggression and hyperactivity (r = 0.11) [[1]](#ref-1)[[20]](#ref-20). Two estimates looked larger, emotional problems (r = 0.30) and peer problems (r = 0.28), but rested on few studies with very wide confidence intervals, so they are the least trustworthy findings, and independent experts warned they are the most likely to be misreported in headlines [[2]](#ref-2). No association was found with executive function (a set of skills including working memory and self-control), and reading, math, and science results were too uncertain to call [[20]](#ref-20). Earlier reviews reached compatible conclusions: consistent evidence of small harms, strongest for sleep, obesity, and low mood, with no clear threshold below which effects vanish [[13]](#ref-13)[[15]](#ref-15).

The overall confidence to place in this is moderate for the *existence and direction* of small average associations, and low for *causation and size for any particular child*. The associations are consistent across countries and decades, but nearly all studies are observational, parent-reported, and blind to what is actually watched [[1]](#ref-1)[[19]](#ref-19).

## Q1: How large, consistent, and causal are the effects? {#q1}

### Brain structure and function {#q1-brain}

**Finding F1: brain-imaging studies find differences associated with heavier screen use, but they are small, cannot show damage or cause, and the more basic measurements find nothing.** {#f1}

*Neuroimaging* means taking pictures of the brain's structure or activity, here with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Two studies anchor this evidence.

A small American study scanned 47 children aged 3 to 5 and compared scores on a screen-use questionnaire (covering access, frequency, content, and co-viewing) with the brain's white matter, the "wiring" that connects brain regions. Children with heavier screen use showed slightly lower microstructural quality in tracts supporting language and emerging literacy [[10]](#ref-10). The study was cross-sectional with a small sample, so it shows co-occurrence, not cause, and it is one of the most frequently misquoted studies in this field.

Much stronger is the ABCD Study, a US project following nearly 12,000 children from ages 9 and 10 with repeated MRI scans. A 2026 analysis examined 2,666 of them across two scan waves and found that parent-reported screen use was associated with the *trajectory* of brain maturation: slightly accelerated microstructural development in reward-related regions, and slightly decelerated development in regions of the control system, which handles self-regulation [[11]](#ref-11). The associations survived controls for age, sex, brain volume, scan site, sibling status, physical activity, and socioeconomic status. Two cautions matter as much as the finding. First, traditional measurements (gray matter density and fractional anisotropy, standard measures of tissue structure) showed *no* association at all [[11]](#ref-11), so the result depends on sensitive new measures. Second, it remains an observational association: families differ in many ways, and the design cannot exclude confounding or reverse causation. No study in this field has shown that screens cause brain damage; the honest summary is that heavier screen use travels together with slightly different maturation curves, for unknown reasons.

### Language and academic skills {#q1-language}

**Finding F2: more total screen time is consistently linked to slightly weaker language, with the clearest evidence in early childhood.** {#f2}

A meta-analysis of 42 studies covering about 19,000 children aged up to 12 found that greater total screen time went with weaker language skills (r = −0.14), and background television (a TV running while a child plays) was somewhat worse (r = −0.19) [[6]](#ref-6). One direction-checking cohort study is important here: in 2,441 Canadian families, more screen time at 24 and 36 months predicted slightly lower scores on a developmental screening test at 36 and 60 months, while lower scores did not predict later increases in screen time [[5]](#ref-5). The effects were small (β around −0.06 to −0.08, roughly a few points on a broad screening scale), and the study measured parent-reported screen time and a screening questionnaire, not full assessments. Still, it is one of the few designs that tested the "perhaps difficult children simply get more screens" explanation and did not find it, at those ages.

At the academic level, the umbrella review found a small negative association with achievement overall (r = −0.15) but no clear associations with specific subjects [[1]](#ref-1)[[20]](#ref-20), and a British review of reviews rated evidence on cognition and education as weak [[13]](#ref-13).

### Attention and behaviour {#q1-attention}

**Finding F3: screen time is linked to slightly more behaviour problems, more for acting-out than for anxiety; the association is weakest in the highest-quality studies.** {#f3}

A meta-analysis of 87 studies covering about 159,000 children aged 12 or younger (average age about 6) found small correlations between total screen time and externalizing problems such as aggression and hyperactivity (r = 0.11) and internalizing problems such as anxiety and low mood (r = 0.07) [[7]](#ref-7). Three internal findings qualify the headline. Correlations were stronger for aggression (r = 0.17) than for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) symptoms (r = 0.09); they shrank in longitudinal studies that controlled for earlier behaviour; and they shrank as study quality rose [[7]](#ref-7), a pattern suggesting some of the association reflects weaker designs.

A 2026 meta-analysis of screen time and ADHD diagnosis found higher odds for children with excessive screen time (odds ratio 1.56, that is about 1.5 times the odds), with a striking age split: in preschool children the pooled odds ratio was 2.40, while in school-age children and adolescents it was 1.16, barely above chance [[9]](#ref-9). An odds ratio compares odds between groups; anything observational in this area is a correlation, and the preschool estimate came from few studies with very high disagreement between them (I² = 97%, meaning the studies varied enormously) [[9]](#ref-9).

### Sleep {#q1-sleep}

**Finding F4: the sleep evidence is the strongest, most consistent, and most actionable in the whole field.** {#f4}

A meta-analysis of 20 studies covering 125,000 school-age children and adolescents found that using a portable screen device around bedtime doubled the odds of inadequate sleep quantity (odds ratio 2.17), and was linked to poor sleep quality (1.46) and daytime sleepiness (2.72) [[3]](#ref-3). Strikingly, children who merely *kept* a device in the sleep environment without using it also had higher odds of inadequate sleep (1.79), consistent with distraction, alerts, and the temptation factor rather than screen light alone. This is a cross-sectional evidence base, but it is large, consistent, and biologically plausible. Figure 2 shows these odds ratios with their confidence intervals.

A 2025 meta-analysis restricted to cohort studies, which follow children forward in time and give stronger evidence, pooled 548,000 participants and found each additional daily hour of screen time associated with about 3 to 5 minutes less total sleep, a 13-minute later bedtime, higher odds of short sleep (odds ratio 1.25), and roughly tripled odds of difficulty falling asleep [[12]](#ref-12). The mechanisms are ordinary: time displaced, mental arousal at bedtime, and light and alerts in the evening. Because sleep itself drives language, attention, mood, and learning in children, sleep displacement is the most credible pathway from screens to poorer development, whatever the direct effects turn out to be.

### Mood and mental health, including the adolescent picture {#q1-mood}

**Finding F5: for adolescents, average associations between digital technology use and wellbeing are tiny, while specific harms (cyberbullying, sleep loss) are well documented; the field is openly split on how to interpret this.** {#f5}

For young children, the internalizing correlation above (r = 0.07) [[7]](#ref-7) is about as close as the mood evidence gets, and it is small. For adolescents, a landmark analysis examined three large datasets totalling 355,000 teenagers using specification curve analysis, a method that computes every defensible way of analysing the data rather than one cherry-picked analysis. Digital technology use explained at most 0.4% of the variation in adolescent wellbeing [[14]](#ref-14). For scale: the association was smaller than that of being bullied (β = −0.21 versus −0.05), roughly the same as regularly eating potatoes, and wearing glasses was *more* negatively associated with wellbeing in one dataset [[14]](#ref-14). This does not mean potatoes harm teenagers; it means the average technology association is extremely small. Figure 3 shows the benchmark comparison.

For a six-year-old, this adolescent debate matters mainly as a warning that the same argument will return at older ages, in a sharper form.

### Why size and causation stay hard to pin down {#q1-causality}

Three limitations recur throughout, and they explain most apparent contradictions (Figure 4).

1. **Confounding.** Family circumstances (income, parental stress, housing, language spoken at home) influence both how much screens are used and how children develop. Studies statistically "control" for measured factors, but unmeasured ones remain. This is why associations shrink in better-controlled studies [[7]](#ref-7).
2. **Reverse causation.** Children with difficult temperaments or emerging difficulties may be given screens more, to calm or occupy them. Madigan's direction-checking study did not find this path at ages 2 to 5 [[5]](#ref-5), but it is plausible at other ages and for other outcomes, and no design in this field fully rules it out.
3. **Measurement.** "Screen time" is a parent-reported total that mixes video chats, cartoons, and homework; it ignores content, pace, and companionship [[19]](#ref-19). Two children with identical reported hours may have profoundly different experiences. This measurement problem both dilutes true effects toward zero and makes blanket causal stories impossible to test.

## Q2: Does what and how matter more than how much? {#q2}

**Finding F6: yes: the context associations are typically as large as or larger than the total-time associations, and they point in both directions.** {#f6}

A 2024 meta-analysis in JAMA Pediatrics did for context what earlier syntheses did for quantity: pooling 100 studies (about 177,000 children aged 0 to 5.99, with 64 observational studies meta-analysed) [[8]](#ref-8). The pattern is clear.

*Negative contexts:* programme viewing was associated with poorer cognitive outcomes (r = −0.16), background television with poorer cognition (r = −0.10), age-inappropriate content with poorer social-emotional outcomes (r = −0.11), and a parent absorbed in their own screen during child routines (meals, play, bedtime) with poorer social-emotional outcomes (r = −0.11) [[8]](#ref-8).

*Positive contexts:* co-use, meaning a caregiver shares the screen with the child, was associated with *better* cognitive outcomes (r = +0.14) [[8]](#ref-8). The language meta-analysis found the same sign pattern: educational programmes (r = +0.13), co-viewing (r = +0.16), and a later age at first screen use (r = +0.17) all went with *stronger* language skills, while total time was negative [[6]](#ref-6).

The ADHD meta-analysis adds a suggestive media-type split: passive TV/video viewing showed a higher point estimate (odds ratio 2.20) than interactive digital media (1.10), although the difference between them was not statistically significant [[9]](#ref-9). The umbrella review likewise found stronger associations for mobile phones than for television, and stronger in adolescents than in younger children [[1]](#ref-1)[[20]](#ref-20).

The honest qualification: most context studies are observational too, so "co-viewing causes better language" is not proven either. But the context results have two advantages. They are larger where theory says they should be (the content is actually what the child experiences), and they are directionally consistent across independent syntheses. For practical rules, quantity is the weakest lever the evidence supports, and content and context are the strongest. Total time still matters, mainly through displacement, especially of sleep [[12]](#ref-12); the context evidence does not license unlimited hours of "good" content, and no study suggests that more screens are better than fewer once activities like sleep, play, and reading are protected.

## Q3: Is this a moral panic? {#q3}

**Finding F7: parts of the public debate fit the moral-panic template well; the underlying evidence does not fit the "just a panic" verdict, and researchers on both sides overstate their case when they claim it does.** {#f7}

A *moral panic*, in the sociological sense, is a wave of public fear about a supposed threat to children's morals or health that runs ahead of the evidence, spread by media and authorities, and later seen as exaggerated: researchers list the campaigns against comics, rock'n'roll, and "video nasties" as the classic cases, and note that each new medium has drawn the same generational fear [[18]](#ref-18). Researchers who study the screen debate have documented real features of the pattern. The term "screen time" itself homogenizes very different activities into a single feared quantity, a classic panic move [[18]](#ref-18)[[19]](#ref-19). Public claims have repeatedly run ahead of the science: brain-imaging findings described as "screens damaging children's brains" when the studies show small correlational differences [[10]](#ref-10)[[16]](#ref-16); book-length claims that phones "rewired" a generation [[16]](#ref-16)[[17]](#ref-17). Independent experts commenting on the largest synthesis explicitly described the surrounding "media and social moral panic" as out of proportion to effect sizes around 1% [[2]](#ref-2).

The other side of the ledger is equally documented. The associations exist, replicate across 64 countries, and are not zero [[1]](#ref-1). The sleep findings are robust and mechanistically ordinary. Content and context effects are consistent and policy-relevant [[6]](#ref-6)[[8]](#ref-8). Specific direct harms to particular adolescents, from cyberbullying to sextortion, are documented in corporate records, surveys, and experiments [[17]](#ref-17). And serious researchers, not only pundits, argue the population-level case: Haidt and Rausch present a "case for the prosecution" with seven evidence lines [[17]](#ref-17), while their critics answer that the same literature shows small, mixed, causally unresolved effects [[15]](#ref-15)[[16]](#ref-16)[[14]](#ref-14).

Where does that leave the framing? Three-part conclusion. First, yes, this debate structurally resembles past media panics: generational fear, homogenized threat, claims outpacing evidence, and the same patterns repeating for each new medium. Second, unlike novel-reading or comic scares, this one sits on a real, replicable, small evidence base, so dismissing concern as "mere panic" is as unscientific as the alarm it criticizes. Third, the researchers most cited by either camp converge more than the headlines suggest: average effects are small; effects are uneven, concentrated in uses that displace sleep and interaction; the biggest harms are specific (bedtime use, predatory content, cyberbullying) rather than diffuse brain rot. That convergence is the most useful thing the moral-panic question yields: distrust both "screens are destroying childhood" and "it's all panic," and watch what the specific evidence supports.

## Q4: What this means for family rules for a child of about six {#q4}

**Finding F8: the evidence and official guidance converge on rules about timing, content, and companionship first, and about total quantity second.** {#f8}

### What French official guidance says {#q4-france}

The French commission of experts ("Enfants et écrans : à la recherche du temps perdu," April 2024), which reported to the President of the Republic after hearing more than 100 experts and meeting around 150 young people, gives an age ladder for families. Translated from the French [[4]](#ref-4):

- Under 3: no screen exposure. (Since July 3, 2025 this is a binding rule in collective childcare, where the previous "not recommended" wording became "prohibited" [[26]](#ref-26).)
- Ages 3 to 6: use should be discouraged, "or at the very least strongly limited, occasional, with content of educational quality, and accompanied by an adult."
- After 6: "move towards moderate and controlled exposure, which takes its rightful place among activities that must be diverse and varied for the development of children and adolescents."
- Later rungs: no mobile phone before 11 (entry into secondary school); from 11 to 13, no internet-connected phone; from 13 to 15, no social networks; from 15, a "digital majority" with platforms of ethical design only.

The commission framed its recommendations explicitly as "non-blaming" for parents, and stressed that industry design practices deserve more of the responsibility than individual families [[4]](#ref-4)[[25]](#ref-25). A child of about six sits exactly at the rung between "strongly limited, occasional, educational, accompanied" and "moderate and controlled": the guidance for this age is deliberately a transition zone, not a licence and not a ban.

### What the medical guidance says {#q4-aap}

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) replaced its 2016 screen-time guidance in February 2026 with a policy statement on "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents." Its most consequential change: no fixed hour cap at all. The AAP argues the digital environment is an "ecosystem" engineered for engagement (autoplay, recommendations, rewards) that families cannot manage by counting minutes, and recommends: choosing high-quality programming, offering replacement activities rather than just removing screens, sharing a family tablet instead of giving a child their own, finding non-screen ways to help with emotional regulation, looking for the underlying causes of heavy use (boredom, social disconnection), and screening for conditions such as ADHD or depression when use is affecting sleep, school, or relationships [[22]](#ref-22)[[24]](#ref-24). The AAP's parent-facing guidance adds that rules about balance, content, co-viewing, and communication are associated with better wellbeing than rules about hours [[23]](#ref-23). The WHO guideline for children under 5 (no screens under 1, at most one hour of sedentary screen time at ages 2 to 4, less is better) [[21]](#ref-21) applies below this child's age and is included for completeness; the WHO itself rated the underlying evidence quality as very low.

### A rule set that follows from the evidence {#q4-rules}

"Strong" here means replicated across many studies or resting on prospective cohorts; "moderate" means consistent but observational; "judgement" means the rule is a reasonable application of the evidence rather than something the evidence directly tested.

| Rule | Basis | Strength |

| No screens in the hour before bedtime; no tablet or TV in the bedroom, especially not overnight | Bedtime device use roughly doubles odds of inadequate sleep; mere presence raises odds; ~13 min bedtime delay per screen hour [[3]](#ref-3)[[12]](#ref-12) | Strong |

Three practical notes follow. First, the hour before bed and the overnight period are where the evidence is hardest: this rule alone does more work than any other, because sleep is upstream of language, attention, and mood. Second, at about six the French ladder still says "strongly limited, occasional, educational, accompanied" for 3-to-6-year-olds and "moderate and controlled" from 6: a sensible reading is one to two short, chosen, shared sessions a day, with the rest of the day screen-free by default rather than screens-on by default. That reading is this report's application of the guidance, not a quotation of it. Third, the strongest documented risks for this age are not "brain damage": they are lost sleep, displaced conversation and play, and content that is too fast, too old, or background noise. The rules above aim at exactly those.

## Limitations of this review {#limitations}

The report inherits the field's limitations: an observational evidence base measured mostly by parent report, a "screen time" category that ignores content and context [[19]](#ref-19), high heterogeneity in every pooled estimate, and publication bias detected in at least one major meta-analysis [[7]](#ref-7). This review was not a systematic review: it prioritized syntheses, large cohorts, and official guidance rather than cataloguing every study, and it stopped searching when new queries returned known material; a systematic review could find studies this one did not. Access limitations: several central articles (including the 2026 umbrella review) were paywalled; where full text could not be inspected, findings rest on abstracts, publisher summaries, and labelled secondary accounts, and specific numbers are attributed accordingly [[20]](#ref-20). The applicability boundary deserves emphasis: most brain-imaging evidence covers ages 9 and up (ABCD) or small preschool samples, so for a six-year-old the neuroimaging findings are indirect context, not direct evidence about six-year-old brains. French official guidance is a policy position informed by evidence, not a measurement itself. Finally, this report supports family rules only; it does not address clinical treatment, school policy, or platform regulation, which were out of scope.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

- **Q0.** Evidence shows small average negative associations between screen use and children's development and wellbeing, most robust for sleep; the evidence base is large but observational, so it supports concern about specific uses rather than alarm about screens as a whole. {#concl-q0}
- **Q1.** Effects are small (typically r = 0.05 to 0.17 in children; at most 0.4% of wellbeing variance in adolescents), consistent in direction for language, behaviour, and sleep, weaker in higher-quality studies, and causal claims remain uncertain; confounding, reverse causation, and crude measurement are the standing obstacles. One direction-checking cohort found screen use preceding slightly poorer development, not the reverse [[5]](#ref-5).
- **Q2.** Yes, with qualifications: content and context associations are as large as or larger than total-time associations and point in both directions; educational, shared viewing is associated with better outcomes; background TV, age-inappropriate and fast content, and screens at bedtime with worse ones [[6]](#ref-6)[[8]](#ref-8)[[3]](#ref-3). Total time matters mainly through displacement, above all of sleep.
- **Q3.** The debate partly fits the moral-panic pattern (homogenized fear, claims ahead of evidence, recurring generational template), but the "just panic" verdict is contradicted by consistent small effects, robust sleep evidence, and documented specific harms. Researchers converge, quietly, on "small averages, specific risks, context over quantity" even while their public arguments diverge.
- **Q4.** For a child of about six in France: fix the timing (screens out of the last hour before bed and out of the bedroom), fix the content (slow, age-appropriate, educational), fix the companionship (watch together, talk about it), and keep quantity moderate and bounded by sleep, school, play, and family life. This matches the French commission's age ladder and the AAP's 2026 shift from hour caps to quality and context [[4]](#ref-4)[[22]](#ref-22).

The single highest-value rule, supported by the strongest evidence in this review, is protecting sleep: no screens before bedtime, and no screens in the bedroom at night.

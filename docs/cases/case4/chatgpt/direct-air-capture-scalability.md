# Direct air capture at climate-relevant scale: evidence and conditions for regional pilot funding

*International evidence review for a regional climate committee · 9 November 2026*

## Executive summary

**Decision in brief.** Direct air capture with durable carbon storage (DACCS) is scientifically feasible and has delivered measured removals, but its contribution to mitigation at millions or billions of tonnes per year remains **unproven**. A public regional pilot may be justified as an *information-producing demonstration*, not as a near-term source of substantial climate mitigation. Funding should be conditional on independently verified **net durable removal**, genuinely low-emission energy without displacing more valuable decarbonization, credible and monitored storage, transparent full-chain costs, community safeguards, and a learning question that cannot be answered more cheaply.

The most recent internationally synthesized operational estimate is only about **1,500 tonnes of gross DACCO₂ stored in 2025**, drawn from registries for two Icelandic plants through October—not 1,500 net tonnes. An earlier estimate overstated 2023 activity by using a plant's maximum design capacity. Actual performance therefore matters much more than promotional nameplates. Independently reviewed engineering studies suggest costs can eventually fall, but estimates of **US$400–2,500 per gross tonne** across published analyses and an IEA **US$500–1,900 per tonne** estimate for current projects are neither fully harmonized nor verified market-wide expenditure. A future **US$100–600 per tonne** is conditional modelling, not a demonstrated price. Life-cycle emissions also matter: a 2026 synthesis reports **net/gross ratios of 23%–90%** in the limited available registry assessments of Orca and Mammoth. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[4]](#ref-4)

A defensible committee choice may be **targeted, staged pilot support**, **deferral** until energy/storage and measurement conditions are credible, or **priority for cheaper emissions prevention and alternative removals**. No international figure establishes that an unspecified region meets the conditions.

## Scope, definitions and evidence method

This is a **structured narrative review**, not a systematic screening of all studies. It draws on peer-reviewed technology and life-cycle syntheses, the 2026 *State of Carbon Dioxide Removal*, the International Energy Agency, project registry-based reporting, and a recent operator disclosure. Searches targeted technology maturity, field output, energy/material requirements, costs, storage and funding risks, with contrary assessments considered. Evidence was assessed for **observed versus modelled** performance, source independence, numerator/denominator, system boundary, and relevance to an unspecified region. The 2025 systematic technology review examines literature largely through August 2024; the 2026 stocktake and operator update extend the operational picture. No independent, public full-chain cost audit of large-scale DACCS was established by this review. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3)

**Definitions.** *Direct air capture* (DAC) chemically separates CO₂ from ambient air. *DACCS* adds durable underground geological or mineral storage. *Gross captured* means tonnes separated from air; *gross stored* subtracts losses before storage; **net durable removal** additionally subtracts relevant life-cycle greenhouse gases and any reversal of storage. A tonne used in fuel, food or other short-lived products is normally re-emitted and is **not** a durable removal tonne. Storage and utilization must never be treated as interchangeable. [[1]](#ref-1) [[5]](#ref-5)

## Q1 — What DAC technologies exist, what has been demonstrated operationally, and what remains unproven? {#q1}

**Answer (high confidence on chemistry; moderate on commercial maturity):** Air separation and geological storage each work, and limited integrated DACCS operations are documented. Routine high utilization, long sorbent life, predictable whole-plant cost, and repeated large-scale operation are not yet established. Four principal families differ:

| DAC family | How it releases captured CO₂ | Evidence status and scaling question |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Solid sorbents** | Reusable solids bind CO₂; moderate heat, often roughly **80–120 °C**, releases it. | Commercially operating small plants; durability, humidity effects, fan energy and uptime remain consequential. |
| **Liquid solvents** | Alkaline liquid binds CO₂; some regeneration routes require heat near **900 °C**. | Technology family assessed at pilot/demonstration stage; heat supply, water and capital intensity critical. |
| **Mineral/calcium looping** | Reversible reactions with calcium- or magnesium-based solids. | Certain variants commercially demonstrated in limited configurations; materials handling and integration at high throughput uncertain. |
| **Electrochemical and other emerging routes** | Electricity or novel cycles regenerate capture materials. | Laboratory to pilot evidence; promised low energy/cost not yet a validated full-chain result. |

*Table 1. Technology mechanisms and maturity; categories are broad and do not certify an individual design.* The 2026 stocktake assigns technology-readiness levels around 9 to mature solid/mineral routes, 6–7 to liquid-solvent routes and 4–6 to electrochemical regeneration; these are **not** scores for gigatonne-scale deployment. [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3) [[5]](#ref-5)

**What actual operation shows.** The 2026 *State of CDR* reconstructs **2025 DACCS gross flow to durable storage of 0.0015 MtCO₂ (1,500 t)** from published registry data for Climeworks' Orca and Mammoth, available to October 2025. Its earlier 2023 estimate of 0.004 Mt relied on Orca's **4,000 t/yr nameplate**, not its achieved output; revised data suggest the actual 2023 flow was about one eighteenth of the 2025 value. The sites were still ramping below nameplate in 2025; another announced facility without traceable verification was excluded. This is more convincing than a company announcement, although based on registry reporting rather than a cross-industry independent plant census. [[1]](#ref-1)

**A newer, weaker-evidence signal:** in September 2026 Climeworks claimed Mammoth delivered **675 net tonnes in January–June 2026**, versus **119** in the same 2025 period, after collector upgrades. The two upgraded containers reportedly improved markedly, but the company disclosed **neither audited plant-wide cost per net tonne nor evidence that all equipment sustained the upgraded performance**. This vendor disclosure is evidence of a plausible learning direction, not independent proof that Mammoth's **36,000 t/yr design capacity** is reached. [[7]](#ref-7)

## Q2 — What limits expansion to climate-relevant scales, including energy, materials, infrastructure and permanent CO₂ storage? {#q2}

**Answer (high confidence that constraints matter; low confidence in a global deployable maximum):** The limiting resource is not ambient CO₂ but coordinated **low-carbon electricity and heat, reliable capture media, buildable equipment, CO₂ transport, injection capacity and permissions**. Modelled global potential is not a schedule of buildable projects.

**Energy at scale.** The WRI uses an *illustrative* future DAC energy assumption of **2,000 kWh per tonne**, not a measured value for every technology; some systems need both electricity and heat and should not be represented as using 2,000 kWh of grid electricity. At that same illustrative energy-equivalent intensity, **1 million t/year × 2,000 kWh/t = 2 TWh/year**; **1 billion t/year = 2,000 TWh/year**. At ordinary industrial scale that competes with electrification and fossil power displacement unless additional clean generation is genuinely available. The peer-reviewed synthesis finds no clear historical trend of declining published DAC energy requirements; large improvement is an engineering goal, not a demonstrated fleet result. [[3]](#ref-3) [[5]](#ref-5)

A source may be contractually “renewable” yet not deliver the climate benefit assumed if its capacity would otherwise displace emitting generation. For evaluation, distinguish **plant accounting emissions** (the electricity purchased) from **system consequences** (which generation or abatement is displaced, including across hours). An energy source with low average emissions is necessary for strong net removal but does not establish zero opportunity cost. [[3]](#ref-3) [[5]](#ref-5)

**Materials, sites and storage.** Sorbents/solvents degrade, require replacement, and have upstream emissions; air humidity and temperature affect capture. Fans, contactors, pumps, kilns, heat exchangers and CO₂ conditioning need manufacturing and reliable operations. Water demand, chemical handling, land for associated power supply and local pollution require site-specific assessment—some solvent designs consume water whereas some sorbent systems may recover it. [[3]](#ref-3) [[5]](#ref-5) [[6]](#ref-6)

Geological storage has decades of experience at project scale and, if well chosen and monitored, low expected leakage; basalt mineralization can lock carbon into rock. But the theoretical geological capacity of **10,000+ GtCO₂** is not accessible storage on demand: the 2026 assessment suggests perhaps **~1,000 Gt** usable after physical and social constraints. Near any proposed region, **injectivity** (the annual rate that can safely be injected), connected transport, groundwater protection, fault/pressure assessment, monitoring, liability and public consent could be binding before total volume is. [[2]](#ref-2)

**Reality check on forecasts.** Facilities operating or under construction imply only **~0.55 Mt/yr of potential DACCS capacity by 2030** *if all reach rated output*, according to the 2026 stocktake; financing, permitting and verification can reduce it. Its high-ambition scenario family spans **<0.1–1.8 Gt/yr DACCS by 2050**, a range of model outcomes—not measured progress or an agreed forecast. The larger of those endpoints would require unprecedented expansion in clean energy, manufacturing, CO₂ storage services and workforce. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2)

## Q3 — What are credible current and projected removal costs, and how sensitive are they to technological and economic assumptions? {#q3}

**Answer (moderate confidence in the present order of magnitude; low in future point estimates):** Costs remain high and apples-to-apples comparisons are unusually difficult. Every quote should specify year, currency, gross or net denominator, annual utilization and whether compression, storage, monitoring, financing and developer costs are included.

| Evidence type | Estimate (US$/tCO₂) | What the number does—and does not—say |
|:--|--:|:--|
| IEA 2025, **estimated current projects** | **500–1,900** | Institutional estimate, not publicly audited delivered cost for all plants; denominator and boundaries need project validation. |
| 2025 peer-reviewed engineering studies, compiled in 2026 | **400–2,500 / gross captured** | Heterogeneous *modelled levelized capture* cost; many omit parts of the full value chain. |
| Optimistic best-design model, published 2025 | **97–168 / gross**; **126–170 / net** | Conditional simulation under favourable technical assumptions, **not an observed project price**. |
| Learning/scale projection in 2026 synthesis | **100–600 / gross** | Conditional gigatonne-scale projection, highly sensitive to learning and energy assumptions. |

*Table 2. Estimates are deliberately **not averaged** because the denominator, system boundary, maturity and model assumptions differ.* IEA also proposes roughly **US$300/t by mid-century** as a possible future level, not a guarantee. The 2025 systematic review sometimes expresses its forecast using a *net* denominator; that value is not interchangeable with the *gross* range summarized in the 2026 chapter. [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3) [[4]](#ref-4) [[11]](#ref-11)

**Why the spread is real.** Capture performance per unit of sorbent and its replacement lifetime drive equipment quantity; electricity/heat cost and carbon intensity drive annual operating and carbon-accounting costs; financing cost, depreciation, capacity factor and unplanned outages change the denominator. The 2025 systematic review found a scarcity of comparable geographically diverse cost studies, and often-missing owner costs, contingencies or transport/storage charges. These explain why headline figures can move without genuine technological learning. [[3]](#ref-3)

Geological **compression alone** is estimated around **US$10–30/t**, while onshore storage may range from a few dollars to **<US$25/t** and offshore storage roughly **US$5–55/t**, contingent on site and scale. Transport can add materially more. These figures come from different system boundaries and should **not** automatically be added to estimates already including such items. [[2]](#ref-2)

**Simple denominator test (hypothetical, not measured).** Suppose a fully specified DACCS system costs **$800 per gross tonne captured**, and storage/life-cycle deductions leave **80%** net durable removal. Then **$800 ÷ 0.80 = $1,000 per net tonne**. If only **50%** remains net, the identical gross capture cost becomes **$1,600 per net tonne**. An investment comparison using $800 while an alternative is priced per *net* tonne is misleading; further cost changes from storage or delays would increase the difference. [[1]](#ref-1) [[3]](#ref-3)

## Q4 — How much CO₂ is durably removed after accounting for life-cycle emissions, energy requirements and storage risks? {#q4}

**Answer (high confidence in accounting principle; low in transferable field ratio):** Verified climate benefit is **not** captured volume. A project has positive net removal only when storage is real and sustained and its cradle-to-grave greenhouse-gas footprint is smaller than the atmospheric CO₂ stored.

> **Net durable CO₂ removed = metered atmospheric CO₂ durably stored − life-cycle GHG emissions (CO₂e) − expected reversal/leakage adjustment.** The accounting period, permanence horizon, treatment of upstream electricity and materials, and uncertainty deductions must be declared. Avoid double-counting stored CO₂ or avoided fossil emissions.

The 2026 *State of CDR* reports **99%–100% of gross captured CO₂ transferred into durable storage** for the limited assessed DACCS records, but just **23%–90% net removal per gross captured tonne**, due largely to lifecycle emissions and varying registry boundaries at **Orca and Mammoth**, both powered by relatively low-carbon geothermal energy. This is **not** a universal performance interval, and the study's 2025 **1,500 gross tonnes must not be relabelled 1,500 net tonnes**. The wide spread despite favourable energy conditions makes independent full-chain metering a prerequisite for funding. [[1]](#ref-1)

Peer-reviewed life-cycle synthesis includes scenarios in which grid-powered DAC has near-zero or even **positive** net emissions, whereas renewable-powered cases are more strongly negative, depending on process and system boundary. Conversely, a **2026 prospective** calcium-looping LCA using industrial inputs models **85%–96% net efficiency in 2050**, especially with clean power and integration; these are plausible technological countercases, **not measurements from a 2050 commercial fleet**. Neither pessimistic nor optimistic cases substitute for local verified energy and material inventories. [[6]](#ref-6) [[10]](#ref-10)

Evidence should include calibrated intake/output CO₂ measurements; separately metered electricity, heat and water; baseline and actual material replacement; construction, transport, compression, storage and monitoring emissions; records of injected quantity and storage integrity; uncertainty bounds and third-party audit. Storage credits must be backed by continuing monitoring, funded remediation and long-term responsibility—not merely a CO₂ sales contract. Using DAC-derived CO₂ for synthetic fuels may substitute for fossil fuels but is **not durable removal** when burned; enhanced oil recovery also introduces fossil-production and accounting risks. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[5]](#ref-5)

## Q5 — What can pilot projects realistically establish, and what evidence, safeguards and performance milestones should determine whether public funding is warranted? {#q5}

**Answer (high confidence in testable-versus-system distinction; committee thresholds are proposed governance conditions):** A carefully designed pilot can reduce uncertainty about a particular configuration's **seasonal reliability, energy use, sorbent degradation, upstream carbon balance, measured storage, full-chain cost and local acceptability**. It cannot, by itself, validate a million-tonne factory, a gigatonne supply chain, widely available additional clean power, national-scale pipeline networks or future unit costs. Capturing CO₂ in a test unit without storing it does not certify durable removal. [[3]](#ref-3) [[4]](#ref-4)

**Proposed performance contract:** demand measured **net durable tonnes**, **MWh of electricity and GJ of heat per gross and net tonne**, CO₂ storage mass balance, annual availability/uptime, sorbent/chemical replacement rate, **fully loaded US$ (or local currency) per net tonne**, data completeness and measurement uncertainty. Pre-agree a baseline and independently reviewed pass/fail targets for each indicator—including a **strictly positive net-removal result** under relevant energy accounting—rather than imposing a universal numerical cost or energy threshold without regional evidence. A **full operating year** is a prudent proposed minimum before claims of seasonal reliability; it remains insufficient for decades-long storage certainty.

| Stage / committee action | Evidence that should unlock the next tranche | Failure or stop condition |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Pre-award: fund design/testbed only** | Published testable learning question; transparent comparator to emissions prevention/other removals; credible additional clean energy; storage capacity and responsible operator plausibly secured; community consultation and safety plan. | Claimed reductions rely on short-lived CO₂ utilization, unspecified storage, or electricity that would foreseeably displace higher-benefit abatement. |
| **Commissioning: conditional pilot construction** | Independently checked capture/storage measurement plan, cradle-to-grave LCA protocol, financial and operating data rights, water/chemical and CO₂ transport safeguards, binding monitoring and remediation finance. | Unable to measure net output or obtain a feasible monitored storage route; material community or environmental risks unresolved. |
| **Operation: pay for verified learning and output** | At least one full seasonal cycle of auditable CO₂ mass balance, real power and heat use, uptime, consumables, complete cost ledger, uncertainty reporting and positive net tonnes; public non-confidential results, failures included. | Net climate result ≤0, missing data, unreported safety incidents, or performance outside pre-agreed tolerances without a credible corrective case. |
| **Replication/scale: separate approval** | Demonstrated improvement on prespecified targets, cost per *net* tonne under conservative operating assumptions, secured larger storage injection rate, power-network counterfactual and demand/offtake beyond public subsidy. | Promising unit performance is generalized to region-wide buildout without energy/storage and financing proof. |

*Table 3. Proposed stage gates are decision rules, not observed technical norms or region-independent law.* Each stage should have capped public exposure, transparent third-party assurance, an exit right and shared learning so benefits survive failure. Private co-funding can align incentives but does not replace proof. [[4]](#ref-4) [[8]](#ref-8)

**Opportunity cost and institutional safeguards.** Judge a pilot on *additional information and options created per public euro or dollar*, separately from immediate removals delivered. Include a counterfactual portfolio: feasible near-term emission reductions and other CDR with similar **netness, durability, monitoring and risk**, rather than making a simplistic dollar-per-ton ranking of unlike activities. The IEA notes first-of-a-kind project support and open-access testbeds can generate public learning; one large purchaser accounted for approximately **65% of 2024 CDR credit purchases**, creating off-take concentration risk. A more sceptical Project Drawdown assessment rejects DAC deployment as a priority because of current low output, resource and financing opportunity costs, and potential fossil-fuel lock-in; these concerns should be tested, not dismissed. [[4]](#ref-4) [[9]](#ref-9)

## Q0 — Under what technical, economic, environmental and institutional conditions could direct air capture achieve a meaningful contribution to climate change mitigation, and what evidence would justify public investment in regional pilot projects? {#q0}

**Conditional judgment.** DACCS could make a meaningful **complementary** contribution if successive plant generations reliably achieve much higher utilization and lower *full-chain cost per net tonne*, the needed clean energy is built without delaying emissions cuts, materials and water impacts remain acceptable, and verified multi-decade geological storage and institutions expand together. The scale of the need is not a license to plan as if the capacity already exists. A published model of climate planning finds that assuming high future CDR and then failing to deploy it can aggravate fossil lock-in and later adjustment costs; this is a **scenario-based warning**, not a forecast. [[2]](#ref-2) [[8]](#ref-8)

**Support a limited pilot** when it answers a material and transferable uncertainty, has credible local low-carbon energy plus storage, independent measurement and publication of failures, pre-set stage gates, and a better risk-adjusted learning case than alternatives. **Defer** when site power additionality, storage or monitoring cannot yet be evidenced, but those gaps are remediable. **Deprioritize** when the proposed funding is mainly justified by speculative nameplate removals, unverifiable credits, high indirect emissions, unresolved hazards, or an opportunity cost strongly favouring available emission reductions. Even a successful pilot **does not authorize commercial-scale rollout** without a new region-specific appraisal.

## Limits and research conclusion

Evidence is unequally strong: first-principles chemistry and actual **small gross stored volumes** are well supported; **delivered net totals, full-chain audited plant costs, site-to-site reproducibility and region-specific consequences** are thinner. The 2026 stocktake's net-efficiency range uses two registry-documented Icelandic facilities and non-identical life-cycle boundaries. IEA cost ranges and prospective peer-reviewed engineering models are not directly comparable; company operating updates remain self-reported, even when consistent with a plausible improving trajectory. New technologies' optimistic LCAs do not establish factory performance, while early critics extrapolating extremely large DAC portfolios also cannot rule out a useful smaller role. [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3) [[7]](#ref-7) [[12]](#ref-12)

No local electricity dispatch, permitting regime, storage reservoir, water availability, budget, vendor or candidate project was specified. Accordingly **no regional facility is endorsed or rejected**. Research was closed when Q0–Q5 could be answered with appropriately qualified international evidence and genuine countercases; additional global searches would be unlikely to resolve the locally dependent funding decision. The central public-value proposition is **verified, shareable learning under strict climate and fiscal safeguards**, not the assumption that building a pilot delivers climate-relevant removal.
